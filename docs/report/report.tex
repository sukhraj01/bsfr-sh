\begin{abstract}
BSFR-SH claims a blockchain-protected, honeypot-driven detection and recovery framework for
ransomware in smart healthcare, reporting 98.98\% accuracy and 0.990 F1 on the BitcoinHeist
dataset. We built a complete, from-scratch implementation of all five of its algorithms, both
of its private blockchains under real pBFT consensus, and the honeypot feature pipeline the
paper specifies but never defines. Reproducing the paper's own evaluation protocol, our best
model reaches 0.9442 accuracy / 0.9697 F1 -- 4.56 points below the published figure; an
eight-hypothesis ablation over every candidate methodological explanation we could identify
narrows, but does not close, a residual gap that tightens from 3.58 to 1.51 points. Beyond
reproduction, fifteen extensions build three analyses the paper never attempts. A
\emph{security} analysis: formal verification of our own session protocol, a pBFT-vs-Raft
consensus comparison, external anchoring against a simulated public chain, a four-tier threat
model, and a measured honeypot data-poisoning attack together with three independent defenses for
it -- a statistical one that catches the poisoning strategy predicted least catchable and misses
the one predicted most catchable, at a measured false-positive cost; a protocol-level
commit-then-reveal defense, correctly built and behaviourally verified, whose measured protection
against that same missed strategy is statistically indistinguishable from zero on our corpus; and
a federated defense exploiting the paper's own replicated cloud-server architecture, which costs
nothing beyond what that architecture already provides but is, on our corpus, only narrowly and
inconsistently useful. A
\emph{detection} analysis: the honeypot pipeline's real end-to-end result, adversarial
feature-space evasion and a partial defense against it tested across two model architectures, and
transfer evaluation against three real malware datasets, one of them genuinely dynamic. And a
\emph{scalability} analysis of on-chain storage growth and throughput. We further identify five
independent defects spanning the paper's evaluation methodology, security argument, and
preprocessing specification. The framework's central idea -- chain-protecting a detector's ground
truth and a system's backups from the same adversary -- is sound; several of the paper's
supporting claims measurably are not, and the extensions built here show that idea can be
attacked, and only partially defended even by mechanisms built specifically to close the gap, in
ways the paper's own security argument never considers.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}

BSFR-SH \cite{wazid2023} proposes a blockchain-enabled defense against ransomware for smart
healthcare networks. Its contribution is architectural: two independent, permissioned
blockchains, one holding encrypted data backups and one holding ransomware signatures and
behavioural features harvested from a honeypot, both committed through pBFT consensus among a
fixed set of cloud servers. The pitch is that an attacker who compromises a protected system
cannot also tamper with the detector's ground truth or destroy the means of recovery, because
both are chain-certified and the chain is Byzantine-fault-tolerant against a minority of
colluding servers. On top of this architecture the paper reports a ransomware detector that
reaches 98.98\% accuracy and 0.990 F1 on the BitcoinHeist dataset, beating four prior published
detectors, and a consensus/backup pipeline that commits 1500 transactions in under seven
seconds.

No artifact accompanies the paper: no code, no declared hyperparameters, no train/test protocol
beyond ``90/10,'' no definition of what a honeypot-derived ``signature'' or ``feature'' actually
is. Reproducing a paper under those conditions means first building the thing it describes
closely enough that its own claims become checkable, and only then checking them. This report
does both. We built the full system -- crypto layer, two blockchains, pBFT consensus, honeypot
sample synthesis and feature extraction, a dual-backend ML detection module, a mitigation state
machine, and a recovery path -- against the paper's own algorithm listings, and we ran its own
evaluation protocol wherever the paper specifies one. Where it does not -- which, as
\S\ref{sec:impl} details, is often -- we designed the missing piece deliberately, documented why,
and kept the design decision separate from any claim about reproducing the paper's numbers: a
gap the paper leaves open is not evidence against the paper, and we treat the two categories,
undefined design and measurable defect, differently throughout.

We state the reproduction result up front rather than at the end. We found \textbf{five
independent defects} in the paper, spanning its evaluation methodology, its security argument,
its preprocessing specification, its reported throughput claim, and the correspondence between
what its framework is designed to detect and what it is actually evaluated on. And its
\textbf{headline detection result does not reproduce}: under the paper's own protocol, our best
model falls 4.56 accuracy points short of the published figure, and a leakage ablation designed
to find an innocent explanation narrows that gap to 3.58 points without closing it; a further
eight-hypothesis follow-up (\S\ref{sec:m76}) tightens the bound again, to 1.51 points, still
without closing it. Neither finding is presented as a failure of this project -- both are
measured, bounded, and reported with baselines. The paper's central architectural idea survives
scrutiny better than its supporting claims do, and \S\ref{sec:critique} says so explicitly.

Reproduction alone does not test the paper's central claim -- that chaining a detector's ground
truth and a system's backups protects both from an adversary with every incentive to attack
either. Testing that claim needs a working system to attack, which reproduction under the
paper's own protocol does not by itself provide: the paper's evaluation runs entirely on
BitcoinHeist address features, not on anything a ransomware operator, a compromised cloud server,
or a network attacker actually controls. \textbf{Fifteen extensions}, built once the working
system existed, supply that missing half, organized into three analyses beyond reproduction and
critique. \S\ref{sec:security} is a \emph{security} analysis the paper never attempts: it
constructs the four-tier threat model \S V's five informal paragraphs never state, formally
verifies our own session-establishment protocol against a Dolev-Yao adversary, measures pBFT
against Raft to ask whether the paper's own deployment needs Byzantine tolerance at all, adds
external anchoring against a simulated public chain so a private chain's operators cannot rewrite
history undetected, and measures a real honeypot data-poisoning attack together with three
independent defenses built and evaluated against it -- a statistical one (a genuine research
contribution, not a critique, that finds the defense catches exactly the poisoning strategy
predicted least catchable and misses the one predicted most catchable); a protocol-level
commit-then-reveal defense (\S\ref{sec:m7-14}) that behaves exactly as designed yet measurably
does not degrade the one strategy it targets on our corpus, for a reason this project measures
rather than assumes; and a federated disagreement-based defense (\S\ref{sec:m7-15}) exploiting the
paper's own replicated cloud-server architecture, costing nothing beyond what that architecture
already provides but, on our corpus, real for only a narrow and inconsistent slice of the attack
space. \S\ref{sec:detection-analysis} is a
\emph{detection} analysis the paper's own \S VII never runs, because it evaluates on data no
ransomware author could ever produce: the honeypot pipeline's real end-to-end result once its
undefined layer is actually built, adversarial feature-space evasion against the resulting
detector and a hardening defense tested across two model architectures, and transfer evaluation
against two real malware datasets the detector was never fit on. \S\ref{sec:scalability} is a
\emph{scalability} analysis of on-chain storage growth at clinical scale and the paper's own
reported throughput claim.

Taken together, the reproduction, the critique, and the twelve extensions answer a broader
question than ``does the published number replicate'': does the architecture BSFR-SH proposes
survive contact with the adversaries it claims to defend against, once it is actually built and
attacked. \S\ref{sec:critique} answers the first question; \S\ref{sec:security} through
\S\ref{sec:scalability} answer the second.
\section{Paper Summary}

BSFR-SH defines five algorithms across two independent blockchains, $BC_{DTBU}$ (data backups)
and $BC_{SigRW}$ (ransomware signatures and features), each committed by a four-node pBFT
\cite{castro-liskov} cluster ($n=4$, tolerating $f=1$ Byzantine replica, commit threshold
$2f+1=3$).

\textbf{Algorithm 1 (backup creation).} A protected system $SYS_i$ ships its backup $DT_{BU}$ to
a cloud server $CS_l$ over an established session key. $CS_l$ encrypts it into transactions
$E_{KU}(Tx_m)$, assembles a block, and the pBFT leader drives consensus to append it to
$BC_{DTBU}$.

\textbf{Algorithm 2 (data collection).} A honeypot $HP_{RW}$ is deployed and harvested; raw data
$DT_{RW}$ is cleaned to $DT_{RWC}$, from which a signature $Sig_{RW}$ and a feature vector
$FT_{RW}$ are ``generated'' (the paper's own word, and its only specification of either); the
result is encrypted, blocked, and committed to $BC_{SigRW}$ through the same pipeline shape as
Algorithm 1.

\textbf{Algorithm 3 (detection).} A detection module $DM_{CS_l}$ decrypts $BC_{SigRW}$, trains
four classifiers -- random forest, logistic regression, decision tree, $k$-nearest-neighbours --
builds a normal profile $NProf$ and an abnormal profile $AProf$, and runs continuous detection.

\textbf{Algorithm 4 (mitigation).} On a positive detection, $SYS_i$ is isolated and remediated
by one of three cases: Case-1 erases the ransomware in place; Case-2 formats the system and
restores it via Algorithm 5; Case-3, if the demanded ransom is less than the assessed value of
the data, pays it and requests the decryption key.

\textbf{Algorithm 5 (recovery).} $SYS_i$'s backups are located in $BC_{DTBU}$, decrypted by the
cloud server holding the wrapping key, relayed through a second server, and delivered back to
$SYS_i$.

\textbf{Section V} argues five security properties informally: mutual-authentication session
keys defeat replay/MITM/impersonation; deleted post-registration credentials defeat
privileged-insider and stolen-verifier attacks; pBFT resists 51\% attacks, selfish mining, and
(in a permissioned deployment) Sybil attacks; chain immutability resists DoS, manipulation, and
leakage; and the two-chain split isolates detection from recovery.

\textbf{Section VII} evaluates the ML component on the BitcoinHeist Ransomware Address Dataset
\cite{akcora2021bitcoinheist} (2{,}916{,}697 rows, 1.42\% ransomware), resampled to a 90\%
ransomware / 10\% benign split, reporting accuracy and F1 for all four classifiers and comparing
the best against four prior published detectors. It separately benchmarks consensus and block
construction on both chains at three cases (5/10/15 blocks $\times$ 100 transactions) on a
single Windows machine (Intel i5 9th gen, 8~GB RAM) running four miner-node processes in Java,
reporting wall-clock time and transactions-per-second for each case and chain.

Neither evaluation states what a reproduction would need to check it independently. Section VII
gives no hyperparameters for any of the four classifiers, no cross-validation scheme, and no
random seed; ``90/10 split'' is the entire description of the sampling protocol. The timing
benchmark states block and transaction counts but not a single transaction's payload size, so
every second in Fig.~6 is, on the page, a function of an unstated constant. Both gaps are
filled explicitly in our implementation (declared hyperparameters, declared payload size, a
swept sensitivity range) rather than guessed once and left implicit, so that our own reported
numbers are reproducible from our own configuration even where the paper's are not.
\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

The paper leaves several parts of its own design unspecified. Our rule throughout was to fill
each gap deliberately and document the rationale rather than silently ``fixing'' the paper, so
that every departure from the published design is a recorded decision, not an unremarked
substitution. This section covers six of those design decisions, chosen because each one is
load-bearing for whether the framework, once built, actually does what the paper says it does.

The scope of what had to be built to reach that point is worth stating once, here, before the
individual decisions: a full crypto layer (ECDSA, AEAD, hybrid key wrapping, a Merkle tree with
its second-preimage weakness closed, a mutual-authentication session protocol), two independent
blockchain implementations under a real four-node pBFT cluster with view-change on leader
timeout, a honeypot sample synthesizer and feature extractor, a dual-backend detection module,
a mitigation state machine, and a recovery path -- exercised by an adversarial test suite that
includes tampered blocks, wrong signatures, colluding and equivocating Byzantine replicas, replay
and man-in-the-middle attempts against the session protocol, and Sybil identity swarms against
the permissioned membership. Every module in this section is one piece of that whole; the whole
is what makes the honeypot pipeline's end-to-end result in \S\ref{sec:honeypot-eval}
possible at all.

\subsection{Hybrid encryption for transaction payloads}

Algorithm 1 line 2 and Algorithm 2 line 7 write transaction payloads as $E_{KU_{CS_l}}(Tx)$ --
encrypted directly under the receiving cloud server's public key. Elliptic-curve public-key
encryption is not designed to carry bulk payloads, and healthcare backups are not small. We
implement standard hybrid encryption instead: AES-256-GCM over the payload, with the
symmetric data key wrapped by ECIES under $KU_{CS_l}$. The semantics are unchanged -- only
$CS_l$ can decrypt -- but the substitution introduces a seam the original single-primitive
scheme does not have. A wrapped key and the payload it protects are now two separate byte
strings inside one transaction, and if they are merely adjacent rather than bound, an attacker
who forges nothing can lift the wrapped key from one transaction and attach it to another's
payload: every block signature still verifies, because the signature covers whatever bytes the
block happens to contain, while $CS_l$ ends up decrypting data the honest sender never
submitted. That defeats the paper's own \S V-4 data-manipulation claim without breaking a single
cryptographic primitive. We close it by binding the wrap's authenticated data to the
transaction's metadata, and the payload's authenticated data to that metadata \emph{plus} the
wrapped-key bytes, so separating either half breaks a GCM authentication tag.

\subsection{Mutual authentication and session establishment}

Section IV-A and \S V-1 defer session establishment to ``any standard mutual authentication and
key establishment mechanism.'' We designed one: ECDH over secp256r1, ECDSA-signed transcripts
with RFC~6979 deterministic nonces \cite{rfc6979} (the same primitive the paper already
mandates for signatures), timestamps, and message nonces. Implementing it surfaced two defects
in our own first draft, either of which would have made \S V-1's claims false rather than merely
unspecified. First, the initiator's signature did not name the intended responder, so a captured
session opening addressed to one cloud server replayed verbatim as a genuine-looking request to
a different one -- impersonation requiring no key material at all. Second, a timestamp
acceptance window bounds only how \emph{long} a captured message stays acceptable; it does not
prevent replay \emph{within} that window. We fixed the first by signing the responder's identity
into the initiator's message, and the second with a seen-nonce cache keyed by (peer, nonce) held
for twice the timestamp window. Both properties are now pinned by dedicated replay and
man-in-the-middle tests.

\subsection{The honeypot feature pipeline: defining $Sig_{RW}$ and $FT_{RW}$}
\label{sec:honeypot}

Algorithm 2 states, in its entirety, that a signature $Sig_{RW}$ and a feature vector $FT_{RW}$
are ``generated'' (lines 5--6) from data a ransomware honeypot collects. Nothing else is said:
not what the honeypot observes, not what a signature contains, not what a single feature is.
This gap is consequential rather than cosmetic. Without a defined $FT_{RW}$, Algorithm 3's
detection module has nothing to train on, and \S VII's evaluation silently substitutes an
unrelated dataset instead (\S\ref{sec:flaw2}). Supplying this layer is the largest design
contribution of this project: it is the one piece of the paper's own specification that, once
built, lets Phase 3 actually consume Phase 2's output, which the paper as published cannot do.

Two distinct notions hide under one symbol. We split $Sig_{RW}$ into a \texttt{content\_digest}
(a SHA-256 hash over the sample's canonical behavioural trace -- the malware-signature sense,
used for identification) and an \texttt{attestation} (an ECDSA signature by $CS_l$ over that
digest, a timestamp, and a collector id -- the digital-signature sense, used for on-chain
authenticity). The paper's line 5 conflates a content identifier with a cryptographic signature;
separating them is what makes ``building a signature for a malicious program'' mean something
coherent rather than a category error.

$FT_{RW}$ is a 22-feature, seven-group behavioural vector -- filesystem, entropy, crypto-API,
process, network, persistence, and kill-chain-stage groups -- grounded in the measurement
categories the paper's own comparison detectors report. Two decisions inside it are load-bearing
rather than incidental. First, the kill-chain-stage group is truncated to an observable
four-stage prefix (arrival, enumeration, bulk transform, cleanup). The paper's own seven-stage
kill chain ends notification $\to$ payment $\to$ decryption, stages that only ransomware ever
reaches; an untruncated ``stage reached'' feature would be the label wearing a feature's
clothes, trivially found by every model, and worth nothing as a measure of detection. Second,
because we wrote both the sample generator and the classifier that consumes it, an easy corpus
would report a number that describes the generator, not any detector. We therefore built the
corpus to a stated difficulty. A quarter of every draw ($\text{AMBIGUOUS\_FRACTION}=0.25$) comes
from confusable pairs -- two profiles sharing one identical parameter set -- so those samples
carry no label information at all, putting a floor of 0.125 under the Bayes error before any
other class overlap is counted. Four of the benign profiles (a backup agent, a disk-encryption
tool, an installer, a cleanup utility) are deliberately ransomware-shaped, and every counter is
noised, with whole sensor groups going unobserved at a rate independent of the label. The corpus
therefore carries a stated intended ceiling, $\text{EXPECTED\_BAYES\_ACCURACY}\approx 0.85$,
against which any later detector result must be read: far above it is a leak, not a success.

The leakage tests this difficulty budget requires caught a real problem before the corpus
shipped. An early parameter set put a single feature, extension-change rate, at 0.885
separability on its own -- an unintended single-feature giveaway, exactly what a test requiring
every feature and group mean to stay under 0.90 separability exists to catch. After retuning,
the most separable single feature on the committed corpus is extension-change rate at 0.816,
followed by rename rate (0.803) and observed-stage count (0.796); a simple Gaussian baseline
trained on the training draw and scored on the held-out evaluation draw reaches 0.830 balanced
accuracy -- comfortably under the intended 0.85 ceiling, which is the point, not a shortfall.

\subsection{Preprocessing: what ``remove the abnormalities'' cannot mean}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

Algorithm 2 line 3 pre-processes honeypot data and, without qualification, ``removes the
abnormalities.'' Read literally as statistical outlier removal, the instruction is
self-defeating on exactly this corpus: ransomware behaviour is the minority class by
construction -- it \emph{is} the abnormality -- so an outlier-removal step deletes the samples
the detector exists to learn from. Applied naively, Algorithm 2's own pre-processing would hand
Algorithm 3 a clean-looking dataset with the positive class silently gone, and \S VII would then
report a plausible accuracy computed on data that never saw an attack. \S\ref{sec:flaw-preprocess}
develops this as a critique; here we record the design decision it forces. We read
``abnormalities'' as \emph{sensor defects}, never unusual behaviour, and implement five explicit
rules: drop structurally empty records (no observed stage, or a non-positive duration); clamp
impossible values to their physical range, but mark a negative \emph{count} unreadable rather
than clamp it, because ``the sensor returned nonsense'' and ``the program did nothing'' are
different facts that must not collapse into the same number; deduplicate byte-identical traces;
mark missing counters as missing rather than impute them, so the honeypot's blind spots stay
visible to later stages instead of being hidden inside invented data; and normalise absolute
counts into per-second rates. No outlier filtering, smoothing, or class rebalancing is performed.
A dedicated test pins the property that matters: cleaning never removes the positive class.

\subsection{Per-system backup index}

Algorithm 5, lines 1--2, imply a linear scan of $BC_{DTBU}$ to find one system's backups --
$O(\text{chain length})$. We add a per-system index, maintained by the key-holding cloud server
on every block append (\texttt{system\_id} is itself encrypted, so only that server can build
it), reducing lookup to $O(1)$ while preserving the paper's semantics exactly: a stale or cold
index falls back to a full scan, and the index stores only pointers, never plaintext, so restore
still re-reads and re-verifies every block it points to. This moves the decryption cost from
recovery time to append time; it does not remove that cost. Index maintenance is kept explicitly
outside Algorithm 1's timed path, since the paper's own Algorithm 1 has no such index and
folding ours into the benchmark would silently advantage our own Fig.~6(a) numbers against the
paper's.

\subsection{Case-3: declining automated ransom payment}

Algorithm 4 line 7 specifies that when the demanded ransom is less than the assessed value of a
system's data, the framework pays it and retrieves the decryption key -- fully automated. We
decline to implement the payment path. The paper's own \S II-C admits there is no guarantee the
key arrives or works even if payment is made, and automating ransom payment to an active
attacker is sanctioned conduct in several jurisdictions independent of whether it works. What we
build instead preserves the control-flow contribution without the harmful capability: the branch
evaluates the paper's own condition, emits a \texttt{PolicyDecision} object, writes exactly one
audit log record, and terminates in a simulated \texttt{POLICY\_BLOCKED} state regardless of the
decision's outcome. No network access, wallet interaction, or transaction construction exists
anywhere in the module that implements this, enforced by a test that statically scans it for
exactly those imports and asserts the function's only observable effect is the one log record.

\subsection{M7-6 -- three more hypotheses; the bound tightens to 1.51 points}
\label{sec:m76}

Q10 left the gap "unexplained" after testing only splitting and \texttt{address}. This section
tests three further undisclosed methodological choices -- decision-tree splitting criterion (H1),
resample strategy (H2), and single-split variance versus cross-validation (H3) -- plus year/day
feature engineering (H4) and a programmatically-assembled stacked worst case (H5), each on the
same 46{,}014-row (or, where the hypothesis itself resizes the resample, differently-sized)
90/10-style resample and the same declared hyperparameters. Full numbers: `RESULTS.md` "M7-6".

\textbf{H1 -- splitting criterion does not explain the gap.} Entropy beats gini by
$\sim$0.4 points; a decision tree with `max\_depth=None` etc.\ set explicitly is bit-identical to
scikit-learn's own default (asserted in the run, not assumed). Best cell: 0.9301, 5.97 points
short.

\textbf{H2 -- resample strategy is this session's real finding.} Bootstrap-duplicating the
41{,}413 real ransomware rows up to 180{,}000 (oversampled, $\sim$4.35$\times$ average
duplication) and undersampling benign to 20{,}000, under a \emph{random} split, reaches
0.9690--0.9723 -- closer to published than anything Q10 found. Under a \emph{grouped} split the
identical resample scores 0.8746--0.9363, \emph{worse} than the existing baseline: a 5--10 point
swing between splits, several times larger than Q10's largest leakage signature (+0.92 points).
The mechanism is direct: a duplicated row carries its original's address, so a non-grouped split
routinely places literal duplicates of a training row into the test set, and the model recognises
rather than generalises to them. Undersampling benign to 10\% of the \emph{positive} count
(4{,}141, rather than `paper\_mode\_resample`'s 4{,}601 at 10\% of the output total) scores close
to the existing baseline in both splits -- an arithmetic variant of what Q10 already covered, not
a new mechanism.

\textbf{H3 is closed: neither explains the gap.} Twenty seeds on the production protocol give
random forest an accuracy envelope of 0.9288--0.9469 ($\sigma=0.0050$); on Q10's own best cell the
envelope is tighter still ($\sigma=0.0011$, max 0.9572, 3.26 points short) -- the measured numbers
are highly reproducible, not a lucky draw. 5-fold group-stratified cross-validation agrees closely
with the single-split figure (RF: 0.9421 CV mean vs.\ 0.9442 single-split).

\textbf{H4 rules out year as a temporal leak.} Dropping `year` \emph{costs} $\sim$3 accuracy
points, the opposite of what a leakage hypothesis predicts -- `year` is informative, not leaked.
A `year $\times$ day` interaction gives the best H4 cell, +0.5--2 points over the raw features.

\subsection{Consensus Protocol Comparison: pBFT vs.\ Raft}
\label{sec:5c-raft}

This subsection measures Raft (Ongaro \& Ousterhout \cite{ongaro2014raft}), the standard
crash-fault-tolerant alternative to pBFT, against the identical four-node cluster and benchmark
matrix \S\ref{sec:5c} already established for pBFT alone; \S\ref{sec:m7-4} asks what the
measurement below means for the paper's own choice of consensus protocol.

\textbf{What Raft is, briefly.} Raft replicates a log across $n$ replicas via a single elected
leader. Followers accept whatever the current-term leader's \texttt{AppendEntries} tells them,
subject only to a term check and a previous-entry consistency check; the leader declares an entry
committed once a majority of replicas have replicated it, and that fact then propagates via the
next \texttt{AppendEntries}. Leader election uses randomized timeouts and a log-completeness
voting rule so that a new leader is guaranteed to hold every previously committed entry. Unlike
pBFT, no message in the protocol is signed, and no replica ever cross-checks another replica's
copy of an entry -- correctness rests entirely on trusting the current leader.

We implemented Raft (\texttt{consensus/raft.py}) over the identical four-node cluster, message
bus, and \texttt{ClientRequest} submitter-authentication path as pBFT, differing only in the
consensus protocol itself, and made \texttt{framework.\_block\_pipeline} consensus-agnostic
(\texttt{consensus.interface.ConsensusCluster}, DEV-32) so the same pipeline code drives either
cluster. Log compaction, cluster membership changes, and snapshotting are out of scope, matching
the same kind of reduction pBFT's own view-change implementation already accepted (DEV-20).

hold exactly, independent of chain or case:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Messages are flat per block, at two different levels.} pBFT sends exactly 28
messages per committed block in every case ($140/5=280/10=420/15=28$) -- $O(n^2)$ fan-out
(pre-prepare, prepare, commit, each broadcast) at a fixed $n=4$. Raft converges toward roughly 17
messages per block as the one-time leader-election cost amortises across more blocks
($92/5=18.4$, $172/10=17.2$, $252/15=16.8$) -- $O(n)$ fan-out, plus a second leader-driven
broadcast round to announce the new commit index. At steady state Raft sends about 60\% of pBFT's
message count: a real, measured reduction, but far short of the $O(n^2)$-vs-$O(n)$ asymptotic gap
a much larger cluster would show. $n=4$ is simply too small for the quadratic term to dominate.

verifies every pre-prepare/prepare/commit; Raft's \texttt{AppendEntries}/\texttt{RequestVote}
carry no signature at all, by design (\texttt{consensus/raft.py}). This is the sharpest, cleanest
number in the table, and it is also the number that most directly costs security -- see
\S\ref{sec:m7-4-byzantine}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The timing gap is much smaller than the message-count gap, and that is itself a finding.}
Raft is 7--11\% faster than pBFT across the matrix (e.g.\ case 3, $BC_{DTBU}$: 0.750\,s vs.\
0.843\,s), not the $\sim$40\% the message-count reduction alone might suggest. This is exactly
consistent with DEV-08/DEV-21's existing finding that consensus messaging is a small fraction of
the measured span at zero simulated network delay: hybrid encryption, block construction, and
(with serialization on, DEV-30) message encoding dominate wall-clock time, and shaving a third of
the message count off an already-small component of the total produces a proportionally small
timing change. If the deployment's real bottleneck is cryptographic and encoding work rather than
consensus round trips, switching consensus protocols buys a modest, not a transformative, speedup
-- the timing number the paper never measured turns out to matter less than the message-count
number it never asked about.

\section{Security Analysis}
\label{sec:security}

\S V states five security properties as informal prose and never once says, for any of them,
\emph{what an adversary can do} before the property is claimed to hold. GAP-6 names the direct
consequence -- no formal model, no protocol-verification tool -- and FLAW-5 (\S\ref{sec:flaw5})
names a deeper one: without a stated adversary capability, \S V-3 borrows a 51\% proof-of-work
threshold to defend a protocol (pBFT) whose real safety bound is a third, because nothing in the
paper's argument structure distinguishes a hash-power adversary from an identity-controlling one.
\S II is a ransomware taxonomy (four types, a seven-stage kill chain), not an adversary-capability
model -- it says what ransomware \emph{is}, never what a network attacker, a compromised operator,
or an adversarial machine-learning attacker can \emph{do}. This section constructs the threat
model the paper omits and organizes every security-relevant result this project has produced
around it, evidence first, interpretation last: formal verification of the session protocol
(Tier 1), the pBFT/Raft comparison's security finding (Tier 2), hybrid anchoring (Tier 2--3), and
the honeypot data-poisoning attack and its defense (Tier 2) -- closing with the coverage matrix
that maps \S V's five original claims onto these tiers and the gaps that remain. Full version,
with the reasoning behind every cell: \texttt{docs/THREAT\_MODEL.md}.

\subsection{Four Adversary Tiers, by Capability}
\label{sec:threat-tiers}

Not by which paper section mentions them -- \S V's own flattening of a hash-power adversary and an
identity-controlling one into a single borrowed threshold (FLAW-5) is exactly the failure that
follows from organizing a threat model by section rather than by what an adversary can actually
do. Four tiers, used throughout this section:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tier 1 -- external network attacker.} Full Dolev-Yao control of messages in transit
(intercept, replay, modify, inject, reorder); cannot compromise any node's internal state or key.
\item \textbf{Tier 2 -- one compromised cloud server ($f{=}1$).} Controls one of the four pBFT
replicas -- exactly the fault count the paper's own deployment is sized to tolerate.
\item \textbf{Tier 3 -- two compromised, possibly colluding, cloud servers ($f{=}2$).} Above the
$f{=}1$ bound pBFT actually tolerates at $n{=}4$, and, not coincidentally, at or below the 51\%
figure \S V-3 borrows from proof-of-work (\S\ref{sec:flaw5}).
\item \textbf{Tier 4 -- adversarial ML evasion.} No node compromised at all; a rational attacker
who knows a behavioural detector is watching and adjusts their program's observable behaviour to
defeat it -- a capability Algorithm 3 (\S IV-C) never considers.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Formal Verification of the Session Protocol (Tier 1)}

transcripts, timestamps and nonces, built because \S IV-A/\S V-1 defer to ``any standard mutual
authentication and key establishment mechanism'') is where Tier 1's evidence comes from.
\S V is entirely informal prose -- five paragraphs of argument, no ROR model, no BAN logic, no
tool-checked proof -- for a paper whose author's other work almost always includes one. We
verified this protocol, which is our own design and not the paper's, with

agreement violation in which the two parties finish believing they talked to different
counterparts. That the tool is silent on the fixed protocol and vocal on the broken one is a
stronger confirmation than a clean run alone would be. Two limitations are stated rather than

\subsection{Consensus Comparison: The Byzantine Leader (Tier 2)}
\label{sec:m7-4}

\S\ref{sec:5c-raft} measured pBFT against Raft quantitatively at this cluster's own scale --

controls are exactly the setting where ``do we actually need Byzantine fault tolerance?'' is
legitimate, because BFT's cost buys protection against a specific Tier-2 threat -- an actively
lying node -- that the paper never argues is the one it faces.
\subsubsection{Qualitative: the Byzantine leader}
\label{sec:m7-4-byzantine}

We ran both protocols with one of four nodes silently crashed and, separately, one of four nodes
byzantine.

\textbf{Crash fault (both protocols): commits continue.} With one node's outgoing messages
dropped from the start, the other three nodes committed every submitted block under both
protocols (\texttt{crash\_tolerance\_pbft}/\texttt{crash\_tolerance\_raft}, run
\texttt{20260924T045952Z-2eaa456f}: all three live replicas reached height 3 of 3 either way).
$n=4$ tolerates one crash under Raft's majority rule exactly as it tolerates one Byzantine replica
under pBFT's $f=1$ -- the same \emph{count} of tolerated failures, a different failure
\emph{model}.

\textbf{Byzantine leader (Raft only): commits continue, on different data.} We installed a
behaviour on Raft's elected leader that sends genuinely different, honestly-signed transaction
sets to different followers at the same log index, each claiming the entry is already committed
-- standard, protocol-compliant \texttt{AppendEntries} on both sides, no forged signature and no
bug in either sender or receiver. Every node ``committed'' by its own local rule: all four reached
height 2, but two followers hold block hash
\texttt{ebd2c097fd90b599\ldots} while the third holds \texttt{a6cf98b3bdd0447e\ldots} -- two
different, individually valid, individually well-linked blocks at one height
(\texttt{raft\_byzantine\_fork}, same run; reproduced as a standing regression test in
\texttt{tests/unit/test\_raft\_byzantine.py}). Neither side's chain integrity check catches this,
because both blocks are genuinely well-formed; the check that would catch it -- cross-referencing
what a majority of \emph{other} replicas independently attest to -- is exactly the check pBFT's
$2f+1$ matching-vote quorum performs and Raft's leader-trust model does not.

pBFT resists the equivalent attempt structurally: two followers committing different content at
one height would require $2f+1$ matching votes for \emph{each} side, which needs more faulty
replicas than $f=1$ permits at $n=4$ (\S\ref{sec:flaw5} already demonstrates the actual threshold
is two colluding replicas, not one).

\subsubsection{The trade-off, stated precisely}

Raft is 7--11\% faster and sends roughly 60\% as many consensus messages as pBFT at this cluster
size, but a single compromised leader corrupts the chain undetectably -- every node individually
believes consensus succeeded, and nothing in Raft's protocol or in \texttt{Chain}'s own validation
(Merkle root, signature, linkage) notices the fork. Whether that trade-off is acceptable depends
entirely on whether the deployment's threat model includes active server compromise, not merely
server failure -- a question \S V-3 never asks, because it substitutes a borrowed 51\% figure for
an actual threat-model argument (\S\ref{sec:flaw5}). For a smart-healthcare deployment whose own
threat model (\S II) explicitly includes a compromised or malicious cloud server, that gap is not
a minor implementation detail: it is the entire reason pBFT, not Raft, is the correct choice here
-- but it is a reason the paper never states, and the 7--11\% speed cost of stating it and paying
for it honestly is modest.

\subsection{Hybrid Anchoring}
\label{sec:hybrid}

\S VIII of the paper itself states its own limitation and stops: ``in future, we have plan to
work with hybrid blockchain.'' No design follows. \S IV-A already names the trade-off this answers
without resolving it: a private, permissioned chain is fast and keeps healthcare data
confidential, but its integrity rests entirely on trusting the four cloud servers that operate
it -- nothing in the paper's design lets anyone outside that set confirm $BC_{DTBU}$ or
$BC_{SigRW}$ have not been silently rewritten. A public chain would give that external
verifiability, at the cost of exposing whatever it carries to everyone. A hybrid design keeps the
paper's own private chains exactly as built -- same speed, same confidentiality, \S IV's pBFT
consensus unchanged -- and adds one small, public commitment alongside them.

\subsubsection{Architecture: an anchor chain and the \texttt{AnchorRecord} schema}

An \texttt{AnchorRecord} carries one private block's height, its header hash $HC_{\beta_j}$, its
Merkle root $MTR$, a timestamp, and the anchor creator's ECDSA signature over all of it -- copied
verbatim from the private block's own header, never derived from, or requiring decryption of, its
transactions. Every other transaction in this codebase is $E_{KU_{CS_l}}(Tx)$ (Alg.~1 line~2 /
Alg.~2 line~7), sealed so only one cloud server can read it; an anchor record inverts that
deliberately -- its only job is to be readable by anyone, so it is never encrypted.

The anchor chain is simulated, not real Ethereum or Bitcoin, and is modelled as a second,
independent \texttt{Chain} instance with its own consensus. That consensus is direct
sign-and-append by a single authority, not a second pBFT cluster: a single signer has no other
replica to convince, so pBFT's message rounds would be pure overhead with nothing underneath them.
This was a correction, not the first design -- an earlier version ran the anchor chain through a
literal single-node pBFT cluster, which worked, but pulled a dependency from the blockchain layer
up into consensus/framework code that this project's own architectural boundary (enforced by a
standing test, not merely documented) correctly rejected. The point of the exercise was always the
\emph{interface} a hybrid chain needs from its anchor -- commit, and answer whether a given block
still matches what was committed -- not the anchor's own Byzantine fault tolerance, which a real
public chain supplies for itself. Full rationale in \texttt{docs/DEVIATIONS.md} DEV-33.

Given an anchor record and a candidate block claiming to be the one it covers, anyone can check
its hash and Merkle root against the record without decrypting a single transaction inside it.
That is the whole guarantee: the private chain's shape at that height is fixed the moment the
anchor commits, even against an operator who controls every private-chain replica. It is not a
claim about the private chain's contents being correct -- only that they cannot change afterward
without the change being visible to anyone holding the anchor.

\subsubsection{The anchor frequency trade-off}

\texttt{AnchorPolicy.frequency} is the cost/integrity lever: anchoring every block gives maximum
integrity at maximum anchor traffic; anchoring every $N$th block cuts that traffic by $N\times$ at
the cost of up to $N{-}1$ blocks' unanchored tampering window. Table~\ref{tab:m7-5-matrix} sweeps
$N \in \{1, 5, 10\}$ across the paper's own three cases, both chains, serialization on -- the same
condition \S VIII's comparison settled on.

Two findings, both against the frequency lever's intuition. First, anchor overhead is at or below

metadata, never a function of the private block's own payload size. The frequency lever therefore
trades anchor count linearly against the tampering window it leaves open, not against a real
cost curve on the anchor side -- the anchor's own cost is already negligible at $N{=}1$.

\subsubsection{What external verifiability buys: three security tests}

Three tests, mirroring \texttt{tests/unit/test\_hybrid\_chain.py}, demonstrate what the anchor
adds and where it honestly does not reach.

\textbf{(a) Tamper a private block after it is anchored.} A private-chain block already covered by
an \texttt{AnchorRecord} is replaced, on every replica, by a different, internally
well-formed, validly-signed block at the same height -- the same class of attack \S VIII's
Byzantine-leader test constructs, applied here to storage rather than to consensus.
\texttt{verify\_anchor} recomputes the live block's hash and Merkle root on every call and compares
them against the stored record: \texttt{anchored=True, verified=False}. The tamper is caught.

\textbf{(b) Tamper a private block that has not been anchored yet.} At frequency $N{=}5$, heights
6 and 7 of a 7-block chain are not covered by any anchor until a later multiple or a flush closes
the window. Tampering height 6 in that gap produces \texttt{anchored=False}: there is nothing to
check it against, so nothing is claimed either way. This is stated as the honest cost of
infrequent anchoring, not smoothed over -- it is exactly the $N{-}1$-block tampering window named
above as the trade-off's price, demonstrated rather than assumed.

\textbf{(c) Tamper the anchor chain itself.} The anchor chain's own stored blocks are replaced the
same way as in (a). \texttt{verify\_anchor\_chain\_integrity} -- \texttt{Chain.verify\_integrity},
unmodified, applied to the anchor chain -- catches the broken link and the invalid signature, the
same way it would for either private chain. This catches storage-level tampering of the anchor
chain, but rests on an assumption stated plainly rather than hidden: it does not model an attacker
who controls the anchor authority's own signing key from the start. In production the anchor chain
is a real public blockchain with its own, separate consensus; an operator who also controlled that
consensus would defeat the entire hybrid design, not just this check.

\subsubsection{Limitations, stated honestly}

The anchor chain here is simulated, and three simplifications matter for anyone reading these
numbers as evidence about a real deployment. First, a real public chain (Ethereum, Bitcoin, or a
purpose-built one) has real network latency and a real per-transaction cost -- gas fees on
Ethereum, most concretely -- neither of which this simulation pays; the $\approx$1\,KB/anchor
figure above is a storage cost, not a gas estimate, and the two do not convert directly. Second,
this simulation's anchor authority is a single signing key with no consensus of its own; a real
deployment anchoring to a public chain submits one signed transaction per anchor to that chain's
own, already-running consensus, which is a fair analogue of what is modelled here, but the
public chain's own liveness and finality guarantees are not exercised by anything in this section.
Third, and most fundamentally, the paper's threat model (\S II) never specifies who the external
verifier actually is -- a regulator, a patient, an independent security researcher auditing the
deployment -- and this design does not pick one either. What it provides is that verification is
possible for any of them without a relationship with the private chain's operators, which is the
property \S IV-A's trade-off was missing; deciding who should exercise it is a policy question
this report does not answer.

\subsection{Honeypot Data Poisoning: The Attack}
\label{sec:m7-11}

\S\ref{sec:threat-model}'s Gap 1 named, without measuring, this framework's sharpest irony: a
Tier-2 adversary who controls the honeypot's collection process can feed false training data into
$Sig_{RW}$/$FT_{RW}$, and once that data commits to $BC_{SigRW}$ through real pBFT consensus, the
chain's own immutability -- the paper's central selling point -- certifies the poison as
\emph{tamper-proof}, not as \emph{false}. This section measures both halves of that claim.

\subsubsection{The attack: three strategies, one adversary}

A Tier-2 adversary controlling one cloud server that contributes to $BC_{SigRW}$ can submit
crafted samples. Three strategies (\texttt{detection.poisoning}), each a pure transform on the
training draw, never touching the eval set:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Label flipping.} Relabel a fraction of real ransomware training rows as benign.
Features untouched -- the "benign" contribution is a real ransomware trace under the wrong label.
\item \textbf{Feature poisoning.} Inject synthetic rows labelled ransomware whose features are
resampled from real benign training rows.
\item \textbf{Anchor-point injection.} Inject rows interpolated at the midpoint between the benign
and ransomware per-feature centroids, labelled benign -- teaching the model that the class
boundary itself is benign, pushing the effective boundary toward the ransomware centroid without
fabricating an obviously wrong vector or flipping any real label.
\end{itemize}

Budget is a fraction of the training set's ransomware row count (717 of 1467 rows), swept at
1/5/10/20/50\%, against the committed corpus, sanity-checked at the CSV-path baseline
\textbf{0.8408} (not the chain-path 0.8422; DEV-27/DEV-37 explain the 0.0014 gap).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig11a_poisoning_degradation.png}
\caption{Detector degradation under three poisoning strategies, five budgets each.}
\label{fig:m7-11-degradation}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{The degradation curves: sophistication is not effectiveness}

\textbf{Label flipping is the most damaging strategy, and the only monotonic one.} $-1.5$pt
balanced accuracy at 1\% budget, widening to $-17.3$pt at 50\%, collapsing to exactly
$\mathrm{bal\_acc}=0.5000$ at 100\% -- at full budget the training set has zero positive rows
left, so no classifier can be fit past "always benign," which scores 0.50 on a balanced eval set
by construction, not by any model actually running. This matches the session brief's own
prediction: the simplest attack is the one most likely to succeed.

\textbf{Feature poisoning is mild and roughly budget-insensitive} ($-0.3$ to $-1.5$pt across the
whole range, non-monotonic). Duplicating real benign feature vectors under the ransomware label
dilutes $AProf$ without teaching it anything structurally new -- the injected rows are, feature-
wise, indistinguishable from rows already in the benign class.

\textbf{Anchor-point injection -- billed as the most sophisticated strategy -- is the
\emph{least} damaging at low-to-medium budget, and briefly \emph{improves} accuracy}: $+0.6$pt at
1\%, still $+0.1$pt at 20\%, only turning negative at 50\% ($-1.3$pt). Points at the inter-class
midpoint, labelled benign, act as a mild regulariser at low budget rather than the intended
boundary shift, before enough of them accumulate to move it. \textbf{A designed attack's
sophistication and its measured effectiveness are not the same axis} -- the crudest attack
(mislabel real data) beat the most carefully engineered one across most of the tested range.

\subsubsection{The permanence argument, demonstrated once, for real}

Not simulated: \texttt{scripts/m7\_11\_honeypot\_poisoning.py} builds one real malicious
\texttt{CleanSample} through the same honeypot collection path every other record in this
project uses, signs and attests it normally, then constructs its \texttt{SignatureRecordPayload}
with \texttt{label="benign"} instead of the sample's true \texttt{"RW"} -- label flipping,
applied concretely to one record -- and commits it through \texttt{framework.
phase2\_collection}'s unchanged pipeline to a real $BC_{SigRW}$ cluster running actual pBFT
consensus. \textbf{It committed in one block, cleanly, on the first attempt.}

Algorithm 2 has no counter-move, and this is a code-level fact, not an inference: reading
\texttt{blockchain.chain.Chain}'s full public method list --
\texttt{append}, \texttt{check\_append}, \texttt{create\_genesis}, \texttt{adopt\_genesis},
\texttt{draft\_next}, and read-only accessors -- finds no delete, remove, rollback, revert,
truncate, or undo method anywhere. No code was written to produce this finding; the class was
already in the repository (OUT OF SCOPE for this session: no changes to the chain).

\subsubsection{The detection gap: pre-commit and post-commit}

\textbf{Pre-commit.} The poisoned record passed all five of \texttt{Chain.check\_append}'s checks
-- prev\_hash linkage, Merkle root recomputation, hash uniqueness, ECDSA signature, timestamp-skew
tolerance -- every one structural or cryptographic, none semantic. A correctly formatted,
correctly signed poisoned sample passes every existing validation check, because nothing in
Algorithms 1-5 was ever asked to check semantic content, and this project's implementation does
not accidentally add that check either.

\textbf{Post-commit.} Not built, and named as a gap rather than fixed. Honest nodes independently
maintaining their own feature distributions and flagging statistical outliers in new
contributions could in principle catch anchor-point-style poisoning (it visibly clusters near
the class boundary) but not label flipping (its features are real; only the label lies) -- and
the mechanism itself would need cross-node feature-space agreement, a research problem this
project does not attempt to solve.

\subsubsection{Hybrid anchoring does not help}

\S\ref{sec:hybrid}'s anchor chain verifies that already-committed $BC_{SigRW}$ data has not been
tampered with \emph{after} commitment -- an integrity guarantee over time. Poisoning is not
post-commit tampering; it is a validly signed, validly consensus-approved commitment by an
authorized node in the first place. The anchor faithfully records the poisoned block exactly as
submitted. It cannot distinguish a legitimate record from a false one an authorized party chose
to submit, because that distinction is \emph{validity}, not \emph{integrity} --
\S\ref{sec:threat-model}'s Trust Assumption 2 already states the anchor chain's guarantee stops
there.

\subsubsection{Not a BSFR-SH-specific bug}

Any system that trains a model on data from a source it does not fully trust, and commits that
data to tamper-evident, append-only storage, faces the same structural tension: the storage
layer's integrity guarantee and the training data's trustworthiness are orthogonal properties,
and strengthening the first -- more replicas, stronger consensus, longer chains -- does nothing
to strengthen the second. Blockchain-backed ML training pipelines inherit this tension by
construction, not by implementation mistake. It is the same "chain integrity is not chain
validity" distinction \S\ref{sec:threat-model}'s coverage matrix already needed for
$Sig_{RW}$/$FT_{RW}$'s own integrity-vs-truth caveat -- Gap 1 is that distinction's sharpest
consequence, not a new one.

\subsection{Statistical Poisoning Detection: The Defense}
\label{sec:m7-12}

\S\ref{sec:m7-11} measured the attack Gap 1 named: a validly-signed poisoned $Sig_{RW}$ record
commits to $BC_{SigRW}$ exactly as cleanly as an honest one, because \texttt{Chain.check\_append}'s
five checks are all structural or cryptographic. This section builds and measures the defense
Gap 1 predicted the shape of but never implemented -- a genuine research contribution, not a
reproduction of the paper or a critique of it, since Algorithm 2 has no semantic validation step
to reproduce or critique in the first place.

\subsubsection{The mechanism: batch-level drift, gated into the existing vote}

\texttt{detection.drift.DriftDetector} keeps a per-replica running per-feature profile
(\texttt{RunningStats}, Welford's online algorithm \cite{welford1962} -- no historical sample is
ever stored or re-read) and scores an incoming batch two ways: \textbf{Mahalanobis}, a diagonal,
RMS-aggregated standardized distance of the batch centroid from the running mean; and
\textbf{Page-Hinkley} \cite{page1954}, a classical streaming mean-shift test applied to that same
scalar signal, for slow cumulative drift
across many small batches. A full per-feature Kolmogorov-Smirnov test needs the historical sample
\emph{values}, not just their moments, so it was scoped out rather than built. This is
deliberately \emph{not} per-sample outlier detection -- DEV-26 already rules that out for the
honeypot's own design, since a single unusual sample can be genuine, newly-observed ransomware
behaviour, not poison.

\texttt{consensus.validated\_commit.ValidatedSigRWChain} subclasses \texttt{Chain} and overrides
\texttt{check\_append()} -- the one place \texttt{consensus/pbft.py}'s own docstring says block
validity is decided ("Consensus decides whether, \texttt{Chain} decides valid") -- to run the
detector over the block's decrypted $Sig_{RW}$ batch after the five existing checks pass. A
replica that detects drift raises \texttt{ChainError} and is rejected by the \emph{existing} pBFT
vote-counting exactly like a bad signature would be: no new consensus message, no change to
\texttt{Replica}'s state machine. \texttt{Cluster.\_\_init\_\_} gained one additive,
backward-compatible \texttt{chain\_factory} parameter (default \texttt{Chain}) so $BC_{DTBU}$'s
commit path is unaffected.

\subsubsection{Defended vs.\ undefended: all 15 of \S\ref{sec:m7-11}'s cells}

\texttt{label} are separate fields by design), but because an all-malicious batch's feature

\subsubsection{False-positive rate, and the new-family tension}

Zero of 30 chunked, never-poisoned eval-corpus batches (25 rows each) triggered a false alarm at
the shipped threshold. A documented, disclosed feature-space perturbation of real ransomware rows
-- scaling network/beacon features up and crypto/encryption features down, modelling a
hypothetical family that exfiltrates rather than encrypts, never a new call into
\texttt{honeypot/collector.py}'s own generator -- scored $1.2255$, roughly $17\times$ its own
matched clean-batch $p_{95}$ ($0.070$), and was \emph{still not flagged} at the shipped threshold.
This is the tension item 4 of the M7-12 brief asked for, measured rather than assumed: a threshold
loose enough to catch that probe sits close enough to the label-flip/feature-poison noise range
that it would also raise the false-positive rate on ordinary small clean batches -- the exact
trade-off a real deployment would have to accept explicitly, not one this session resolved by
picking a more flattering number.

\subsubsection{The architectural observation, and its cost}

Gating the check inside \texttt{check\_append()} is the right integration point because it is the
last moment before immutability makes the decision permanent -- once committed, $BC_{SigRW}$'s own
guarantee (\S\ref{sec:m7-11}) prevents removal. Reusing pBFT's existing $2f{+}1$ threshold, rather
than inventing a new vote type, means a Tier-2 adversary who also controls one consensus replica
still cannot force a commit past three independently-refusing honest replicas.

That integration point has a cost this project had not paid before: \texttt{blockchain/
transaction.py}'s own docstring states "miners validate blocks they cannot read; that is the
point" -- $Sig_{RW}$ payloads are hybrid-encrypted to one recipient (DEV-01), and no replica held
that key before this session. \texttt{ValidatedSigRWChain} requires one, which
\S\ref{sec:threat-model} states as a new trust assumption (TA-6) rather than a detail: this
defense narrows a confidentiality invariant that held since M2b. A second new assumption (TA-7):
every honest replica must run the identical \texttt{DriftPolicy} to agree on whether to vote --
untested against a policy-mismatched replica, stated as a limitation rather than assumed away.

\subsubsection{The gap is real, and this is a measured, partial answer to it}

This defense is not in the paper's design, and its absence is a real gap -- \S\ref{sec:m7-11}
demonstrated exactly how much that costs. It is also not a general fix: measured against the
three strategies and five budgets already on record, it closes one case (a batch introducing
feature values the corpus profile has never produced, submitted at enough scale against a mature
history) and does not close the strategy M7-11 found most damaging, except as a side effect of
batch composition an honest outbreak would trigger identically. A single record -- \S\ref{sec:m7-11}'s
own permanence-demo shape -- is undetectable in principle at any threshold, since a batch of one
has no averaging to suppress sampling noise. Reporting a solvable gap honestly, with the numbers
that show exactly how far the solution reaches and where it stops, is the more useful contribution
than reporting either "poisoning is unsolved" or "poisoning is solved."

\subsection{Commit-Then-Reveal: A Protocol-Level Defense}
\label{sec:m7-14}

\S\ref{sec:m7-12} explained \emph{why} statistical drift detection cannot catch anchor-point
injection: its engineered midpoint is computable directly from the current round's honest
distribution, and in a roughly class-balanced corpus that midpoint sits almost exactly at the
population mean. The fix that finding points at is protocol-level, not statistical: deny the
adversary that visibility before it commits. \texttt{consensus.commit\_reveal} implements a
two-phase commit/reveal/merge protocol -- each contributing node commits to
$H(\mathrm{batch}_l \Vert r_l)$ over its \emph{entire} batch (every feature value, via
\texttt{SignatureRecordPayload.to\_bytes()} -- a count-only or label-only commitment would let an
adversary commit to a size and fill in anything at reveal) before any other node's contribution
is visible; reveals are verified against their commitments, and a node that withholds or reveals a
mismatch is excluded from the round, reported rather than raised, so one bad participant cannot
abort an otherwise-healthy round. \texttt{framework.commit\_reveal\_pipeline} hands the merged,
verified batch to the existing block pipeline unmodified -- \texttt{consensus/pbft.py} and
\texttt{\_block\_pipeline.py} are byte-for-byte unchanged.

\textbf{The protocol behaves exactly as specified.} Commitment tamper detection, round exclusion
(non-reveal, mismatch, missing commitment), canonical deterministic merging, and \texttt{f=1}
crash tolerance through a real pBFT commit are all measured directly (22 unit tests, one
real-consensus integration test). An always-withholding adversary is permanently excluded at
exactly round 3 of a 6-round simulation, matching the configured threshold precisely.

\textbf{Its measured effect on anchor-point injection's damage is statistically indistinguishable
from zero on this corpus.} Constraining the adversary to a historical-only view (85\% of the
corpus, the current round's honest 15\% withheld until reveal) and re-running M7-11's exact
three-strategy, five-budget sweep: \texttt{damage\_prevented} for anchor-point injection stays
within a hundredth of a point at every budget ($+0.0026$, $+0.0000$, $+0.0056$, $-0.0000$,
$+0.0026$). An 8-repeat robustness check (independent historical-split and injection-draw seeds)
confirms this is not one lucky split -- the mean sits within one standard deviation of zero at
every budget ($-0.0013$ to $+0.0055$, $\sigma=0.0010$--$0.0034$) -- and a 15\%-historical
("early-chain") sensitivity check, an order of magnitude less historical data, shows the identical
null pattern. \texttt{label\_flip}/\texttt{feature\_poison} show larger, noisier swings (up to
$-0.0325$ in one run), but this is sampling-pool noise, not a protocol effect: neither strategy
needs this round's honest data to operate in the first place (\texttt{label\_flip} relabels the
adversary's own collected samples; \texttt{feature\_poison} resamples from whatever benign pool it
has), so commit-reveal was never expected to constrain them.

\textbf{Why the null result, measured rather than assumed:} \texttt{honeypot.collector}'s profiles
are stationary, low-dimensional (22-feature) mixtures (DEV-27) -- a historical sample of only
$\approx$106 positive rows already estimates the population centroid almost as precisely as the
full $\approx$700-row corpus would, so withholding the current round costs the adversary almost
nothing it could not already infer from history. Ten sequential commit-reveal rounds (the
adversary's historical view, including its own prior poison, growing each round) show no
monotonic strengthening either -- the same mechanism, the same null result. Combined with M7-12's
drift detector: every defended anchor-point cell scores $0.012$--$0.017$, barely above M7-12's own
undefended $0.008$--$0.016$, both two orders of magnitude below the $3.0$ alarm threshold --
nothing is newly caught, consistent with the estimate barely having moved.

\textbf{Collusion is bounded, not merely deferred.} Two colluding nodes sharing batches before
committing would restore the adversary's visibility, but at $n{=}4$ this requires corrupting a
second replica, which already exceeds pBFT's own $f<n/3$ tolerance (\S\ref{sec:threat-tiers}).
Commit-reveal therefore adds no new trust assumption for this failure mode beyond consensus's
existing one -- it does add one genuinely new assumption elsewhere (\S\ref{sec:threat-model},
Trust Assumption 8): a committing node must not leak its batch out-of-band before revealing,
which no cryptographic commitment can prevent on its own.

\textbf{Honest verdict.} The protocol is correctly built, adds essentially no new attack surface,
and behaves exactly as designed. What it does not do, measured rather than assumed, is
meaningfully protect \emph{this project's specific corpus} against anchor-point injection: the
corpus's centroids converge from too little historical data for "the adversary must guess wrong"
to actually occur at any tested scale. The paper's own architecture commits data before validating
its semantic content at all; commit-reveal adds a validation step that uses the protocol's own
structure (who saw what, and when) rather than requiring replicas to understand the data's
meaning -- a defense with real conceptual reach, whose demonstrated reach on this corpus is
narrower than its motivating argument alone would suggest. Full numbers: \texttt{RESULTS.md} M7-14;
mechanism and trust assumption: DEV-40.

\subsection{Federated Detection: Exploiting Replicated Redundancy}
\label{sec:m7-15}

The two defenses above both look at the \emph{data}: \S\ref{sec:m7-12} inspects feature
distributions and fails when the poison looks statistically normal; \S\ref{sec:m7-14} restricts
what the adversary can see before committing and fails when historical data alone already
estimates the boundary. Neither looks at the one thing the paper's own architecture already
duplicates for free: \texttt{docs/ARCHITECTURE.md} \S consensus's four cloud servers each hold a
decryption key and can each independently run Algorithm 3 on the identical \(BC_{SigRW}\) chain.
If one server is the poisoner, its model may disagree with the other three's -- and that
disagreement costs no new protocol message and, beyond the decrypt-capable-replica assumption
\S\ref{sec:m7-12} already introduced, no new trust assumption either.

\textbf{Why replicas trained on the same chain would ever disagree at all.} If every node commits
to and trains on the identical data, there is no obvious source of disagreement. Three scenarios
supply one, each measured against M7-11's three strategies at 5/10/20\% budgets (27 cells,
\texttt{scripts/m7\_15\_federated\_detection.py}):

\textbf{(a) The poisoner trains on a different view than it commits.} Rational for an attacker
who wants to degrade \emph{others'} detection, not its own: three honest nodes fit the poisoned
chain; the poisoner fits its own model on the pre-poison, clean view. Disagreement always
correctly names the poisoner as the outlier node (mechanical -- three identical honest nodes
outvote one differently-trained node every time). \textbf{The irony this scenario is built to
state plainly: the "outlier" disagreement identifies can be the node with the correct model, not
the wrong one -- and this holds for two of the three strategies, not all three.} For
\texttt{label\_flip}/\texttt{feature\_poison}, the poisoner's clean model ($0.8408$) genuinely
outperforms the honest nodes' degraded one ($0.7722$--$0.8363$): disagreement flags the accurate
model as anomalous, exactly the finding the scenario predicts. For \texttt{anchor\_point\_
injection}, the irony inverts -- the honest (poisoned) nodes score \emph{higher} ($0.8425$--
$0.8454$) than the poisoner's own clean baseline, reproducing \S\ref{sec:m7-11}'s own finding that
this strategy sometimes improves accuracy rather than degrading it. Excluding the identified
outlier improves nothing in any of the 9 cells: at a 3-1 split, majority voting had already
suppressed the single dissenter's influence before any exclusion step runs.

\textbf{(b) Algorithm diversity over one shared poisoned draw.} Four nodes, one of \texttt{configs/
ml.yaml}'s four declared algorithms each (RF/DT/KNN/LR), all trained on the identical poisoned
data. Majority voting never scores below its own weakest individual algorithm in any of the 9
cells -- KNN alone is badly hurt on this feature space regardless of poisoning ($0.658$--$0.669$),
and every majority vote recovers to $0.768$--$0.823$. This is \textbf{not a like-for-like
improvement over the M7-11/12/14 baseline}, which soft-votes all four algorithms inside one
detection module (Algorithm 3's own $DM_{CS_l}$); scenario (b) hard-votes four \emph{separately-
fit} single-algorithm detectors, and that architecture underperforms the paper's own blended
ensemble on two of three strategies and is roughly even on the third (Table~\ref{tab:poisoning-
defenses}) -- the paper's own soft-vote design already captures most of what algorithm diversity
would add. KNN and Decision Tree score identical balanced accuracy across all three budgets for
\texttt{anchor\_point\_injection} ($0.6602$/$0.8203$ exactly) -- a measured insensitivity of local
and piecewise-constant models to a small injected cluster that apparently never enters any eval
row's nearest-neighbour set or crosses an existing tree split, not an averaging artefact.

\textbf{(c) A private, never-committed holdout per node} -- this project's own extension, not the
paper's: Algorithm 3 trains exclusively on $BC_{SigRW}$, and nothing in it reserves data before
committing. Four nodes each hold back 10\% of their own contribution, fit on the (possibly
poisoned) committed 90\%, and self-score against their own held-back 10\% -- no vote and no
cross-node communication needed at all. \textbf{Measured result: a weak, inconsistent signal, not
a reliable one.} Of 9 cells, exactly one crosses this session's own stated detectability band
(the mean drop across four independent node-specific holdout splits is positive and exceeds its
own cross-node standard deviation) -- \texttt{anchor\_point\_injection} @10\% ($+0.0149$ vs.\
$\sigma{=}0.0115$), the one strategy both prior defenses measured null against. \texttt{label\_
flip}, the project's most damaging strategy, is \textbf{not} reliably caught even at 20\% budget
($+0.0374$ vs.\ $\sigma{=}0.0554$ -- zero sits inside the band): a $\approx$62--70-row holdout
across only four independent splits does not yet separate real degradation from sampling noise.

\textbf{The architectural argument, and its limit.} Federated detection is the cleanest of the
three defenses in one specific sense: it costs nothing beyond what the paper's own replicated
deployment already pays for, and it needs no new protocol message, no statistical threshold, and
(beyond \S\ref{sec:m7-12}'s existing decrypt-capable-replica assumption) no new trust assumption.

servers do once they disagree, which the paper does not specify and this project does not add.

\textbf{Three of the paper's five properties hold at their defensible tier: (1) fully, (3)
partially (Sybil and $f{=}1$, not the ``51\%'' framing), (5) unconditionally.} Two have measured
limitations the paper does not acknowledge: \textbf{(2) was never implemented}, so it is
unverified rather than disproven -- there is no registration-authority module in this codebase to
test it against; \textbf{(4) is true for manipulation but has a demonstrated DoS gap} the paper
never tests for (DEV-20's reduced view change: one lagging honest replica plus one Byzantine
replica stalls the chain at $n{=}4$, demonstrated in \texttt{test\_pbft\_byzantine.py}). The
sixth row is not a \S V claim at all -- the clearest
form GAP-6's ``no formal model'' takes: a threat model that never mentions adversarial ML cannot
be faulted for failing against it, but a deployment relying on \S V's five properties alone would
have no way to know that attack surface existed, since Algorithm 3 (\S IV-C) assumes $FT_{RW}$ is
simply observed and never adversarially shaped.

\textbf{Gaps the matrix surfaces, stated as findings, not fixed here.} \textit{Data poisoning of
the honeypot} (Tier 2-adjacent, \textbf{measured in \S\ref{sec:m7-11}}): a compromised honeypot
could feed false records into $Sig_{RW}$/$FT_{RW}$; once committed, $BC_{SigRW}$'s immutability
makes the poisoning \emph{permanent and tamper-evident, not detectable} -- chain integrity
certifies a record was written as submitted, never that what was submitted was true.
\textit{Insider threat at the registration authority}: \S
V-2's deletion claim is unverifiable from outside the deleting party and nothing in this project
implements the registration flow to test it against. \textit{Denial of service against consensus}
(Tier 2, measured, not closed): Raft (\S\ref{sec:m7-4}) is faster when honest but is
\emph{worse} under exactly this adversary -- it loses safety, not just liveness, to a single
Byzantine leader -- so it is a different, worse trade-off, not an available fix.
\textit{Side-channel leakage from the encrypted backup chain} (Tier 1-adjacent): transaction sizes
and timing on $BC_{DTBU}$ are not encrypted, and an external network observer can infer backup
frequency and rough data volume without decrypting anything, a structural property of committing
variable-sized encrypted blobs to a chain whose block headers are, by the hybrid design's own
premise (\S\ref{sec:hybrid}), meant to be externally visible.

The paper argues five security properties without stating what adversary each defends against. We
constructed the threat model the paper omits and mapped our verification results onto it. Three
properties hold at their stated tier. Two have measured limitations the paper does not
acknowledge, and a sixth attack surface -- adversarial ML -- exists that the paper's own
vocabulary has no way to name.

\subsection{Adversarial Robustness: The Attack}
\label{sec:adversarial}

\S\ref{sec:honeypot-eval}'s 0.8422/0.8408\footnote{This section trains and evaluates on
\texttt{data/honeypot/corpus\_\{train,eval\}.csv} directly, per \S\ref{sec:honeypot}'s ``committed
corpus is the fixed dataset'' convention. Read this way the same seed and configuration give
0.8408, not 0.8422: \texttt{honeypot/corpus.py} formats every feature to six significant figures
when it writes the CSV, and that rounding alone accounts for the 0.0014 difference from
Table~\ref{tab:honeypot}'s chain-reconstructed, full-precision number. Both are measured; neither
is wrong; every threshold below is relative to 0.8408.} balanced accuracy answers ``does the
detector work.'' It does not answer ``does it keep working against an adversary who knows a
behavioural sensor is watching.'' The paper's threat model is silent on this: Algorithm 3 assumes
$FT_{RW}$ is simply observed, never that a rational attacker -- one motivated to avoid exactly the
detection Algorithm 3 performs -- would adjust their program's observable behaviour to defeat it.
This is not a gap we can hold against the paper alone: testing it requires a defined, differentiable
feature pipeline, which is precisely what \S IV of the paper never provides (FLAW-2). Only having
built $FT_{RW}$ ourselves (DEV-03, DEV-27) makes the question answerable at all.

Table~\ref{tab:top5}'s bounds are not arbitrary. Four features move toward 0 -- an attacker can
always throttle: delay the kill chain so fewer of the four observable stages complete inside a
honeypot's finite dwell time (\texttt{observed\_stages}), rename files less aggressively or not at
all (\texttt{extension\_change\_rate}), slow the rename loop (\texttt{rename\_rate\_per\_s}), or
mimic a common, previously-seen crypto-API call sequence instead of a distinctive one
(\texttt{crypto\_ngram\_novelty}). The fifth, \texttt{write\_entropy\_var}, moves the other way:
ransomware ciphertext is consistently near-maximal entropy (\emph{low} variance across blocks),
while benign writes are heterogeneous, so an attacker raises variance by interleaving some
deliberately low-entropy writes among the ciphertext. Its bound, 6.5494, is not an abstract limit
but the highest \texttt{write\_entropy\_var} the generator's own benign profiles are ever observed
to produce in the committed corpus -- an attacker cannot manufacture more heterogeneity than a
real, non-malicious write pattern in this environment exhibits. No feature in the top-5 turned out
structurally robust (unlike mean write entropy, which cannot fall below the entropy of properly
encrypted data and is exactly the kind of feature that would resist this attack, but did not rank
in the top 5).

\textbf{Single-feature evasion is weak.} Figure~\ref{fig:single-feature} shows every individual
feature, pushed all the way to its bound, costing the detector at most 9 points of balanced
accuracy (0.841 $\to$ 0.752 for \texttt{write\_entropy\_var}, the largest single-feature drop;
\texttt{rename\_rate\_per\_s} alone costs under 3 points). None approaches 0.50. A one-dimensional
attacker -- one who tunes a single observable behaviour and leaves everything else alone -- does
not meaningfully dent this detector.

\textbf{Combined evasion compounds, but does not collapse the detector.} Perturbing all five
features together (Figure~\ref{fig:combined}) drops balanced accuracy from 0.841 to a floor
around 0.57--0.60 by 60--100\% joint perturbation -- roughly triple any single feature's damage,
confirming the features compound as a competent adversary would exploit. It never reaches the
0.50 useless-detector line, though: the top-5 features carry only 45.5\% of Random Forest's total
importance mass (0.126+0.109+0.079+0.074+0.067), so the remaining 17 features, entirely
unperturbed here, still carry real discriminative signal even once the five most important ones
are pushed to their physical limits. This is itself the finding -- exactly the outcome the session
brief's own test criterion anticipated as a possibility (``the model uses features outside the
top-5 enough to still discriminate''), and it is a mild point in the detector's favor: no small,
named feature subset is a single point of failure.

\textbf{Adaptive, per-sample evasion is a different picture.} An adversary who knows the model and
optimizes per sample does much better than one perturbing blindly. Binary search per positive row
over the $L_\infty$ ball (Figure~\ref{fig:adaptive}) finds: median minimum perturbation
\textbf{0.321} (a typical ransomware sample needs about a third of the available evasion range to
flip); 10th percentile \textbf{0.000} -- driven by the 61 of 353 positive rows (17.3\%) the
detector already misses at 0\% perturbation (consistent with the measured 0.8408's own false-negative
rate), plus a further 7.9\% needing under 5\% perturbation, so roughly a quarter of ransomware
samples are trivially evadable by an adaptive attacker; 90th percentile \textbf{0.977} -- the
hardest samples need nearly the entire evasion range. 28 of 353 positive rows (7.9\%) never flip
even at the full $L_\infty$ ball. Checking those survivors against the sixth-through-tenth ranked
features (never perturbed here) shows exactly what the session's own test criterion predicted:
survivors average \texttt{c2\_beacon\_count} 10.3 against 7.0 for the malicious class as a whole,
\texttt{entropy\_delta} 3.58 against 2.61, and similarly elevated \texttt{key\_generation\_events},
\texttt{shadow\_copy\_deletions} and \texttt{dns\_entropy} -- these are the ransomware samples so
extreme on features outside the top-5 that disguising only the top-5 cannot save them.

\textbf{Conclusion: shape (c), the nuanced answer.} No single top-5 feature is fragile enough to
break the detector alone, and no combination of exactly these five is either -- the ensemble's
reliance on 17 further features is real and measured, not assumed. But a median-effort adaptive
adversary needs only about a third of the physically plausible evasion range, and roughly a
quarter of ransomware samples are evadable with under 5\% perturbation on features an attacker can
freely throttle to zero. The paper's implicit assumption that $FT_{RW}$ is unconditionally robust
is false for a meaningful minority of cases and approximately true for the rest, and this
asymmetry -- some samples essentially free to evade, others requiring near-maximal effort -- is
exactly the kind of result a single aggregate accuracy number cannot show. It is a direct
consequence of FLAW-2 (\S\ref{sec:flaw2}): the paper's own evaluation, run on BitcoinHeist address
features that no attacker can \emph{choose their own value of} at will, could never have surfaced
this question, because it is a question about behavioural features an attacker actually controls.
Only the framework path this project built makes it a question with a measured answer.

\subsection{Adversarial Robustness: The Defense}
\label{sec:m7-8}

\S\ref{sec:adversarial} measured the attack. This section measures a candidate defense: if the
ensemble is fit on a training draw that already contains perturbed positives, do the fragile
samples become robust, and what does that cost on clean data? The training draw's 717 positive
rows are each augmented with $K=5$ perturbed copies, one independent
$\mathrm{Uniform}(0,\,\mathrm{budget})$ fraction per top-5 feature per copy, moved toward that
feature's own evasion bound (Table~\ref{tab:top5}) -- negative rows and the eval corpus are never
touched. Three training budgets are swept -- 25\%, 50\%, 100\% of each feature's evasion range --
plus a 0\% degenerate case as a pure sanity check. All perturbation and scoring machinery is
identical to \S\ref{sec:adversarial}'s (`detection.adversarial`, factored out this session so both
experiments share one tested implementation rather than two copies that could silently drift).

\textbf{The degenerate case reproduces the original model exactly.} At 0\% budget, augmentation is
a no-op by design (`detection.retraining.augment\_positive\_rows`) rather than five duplicate
copies of every positive row: duplicating would still change
\texttt{RandomForestClassifier}'s bootstrap sampling for zero new information, breaking bit-exact
reproduction. Measured: \texttt{train\_n=1467} (unchanged), \texttt{bal\_acc=0.8408}, identical to
\S\ref{sec:adversarial}'s baseline to the last recorded digit.

are not noisier; they are more conservative, increasingly refusing to call genuine, unperturbed
ransomware "ransomware."

\textbf{Robustness is non-monotonic in training budget -- the headline finding.} At 25\% budget,
the hardened model is \emph{worse} than doing nothing: median adaptive perturbation to evade falls
from the original's 0.3213 to 0.2499, and the combined-evasion curve
(Figure~\ref{fig:m7-8-overlay}) crosses the 0.50 useless-detector line at full perturbation
(0.463), something the original model's own floor (0.570) never did. A narrow training budget
teaches the model the small-perturbation region at the cost of the large-perturbation region it
never saw during training -- hardening that backfires against a stronger attacker than the one it
was tuned against. At 50\% and 100\% budget the opposite happens, sharply: median adaptive
perturbation rises to 0.8825 and 1.0000, and the 100\%-budget combined-evasion curve
\emph{increases} with perturbation instead of falling (0.736 at 0\% eval-time perturbation
$\to$ 0.888 at 100\%, Table~\ref{tab:m7-8-adaptive}). The mechanism: a large fraction of the
hardened model's own positive training rows sit at or near the top-5 features' evasion bounds, so
the model learns "values at the bound" as a strong ransomware signature in its own right. That is
genuine, measured robustness against \S\ref{sec:adversarial}'s specific attack shape -- but it is
pattern-matching on the augmentation's own footprint, not a deeper representation, and is untested
against any attack that does not resemble "features pushed toward their historical evasion
bound."

\textbf{The "throws away signal" failure mode occurs, partially.} The session's own test
criterion: does the hardened model solve robustness by driving top-5 importance to near zero
rather than learning a robust representation? Top-5 importance sum falls from 0.4553 (original)
to 0.4044 (25\%), 0.2966 (50\%), 0.2309 (100\%) -- a real, budget-proportional redistribution,
never collapsing to near zero. The features consistently gaining importance --
\texttt{directory\_breadth}, \texttt{entropy\_delta}, \texttt{key\_generation\_events} -- are none
of them perturbed in this experiment. This is the partial case the test anticipated: not
"solved robustness by discarding the top-5 entirely," but a real, measured shift of reliance onto
features an attacker could target next, which a follow-up session would need a new perturbation
round against the hardened models' own new top-5 to test.

\textbf{The Pareto trade-off is not a smooth curve.} Figure~\ref{fig:m7-8-pareto}: 25\% budget is
strictly dominated by the original model (worse clean accuracy \emph{and} worse robustness); only
50\% and 100\% trade clean accuracy for robustness in the direction a defender would want, and
100\%'s marginal robustness gain over 50\% (median 1.000 vs.\ 0.883) is small next to its
additional clean-recall cost (0.586 vs.\ 0.632 recall) -- 50\% budget is the more defensible
operating point of the three tested, not 100\%.

\textbf{Conclusion: adversarial retraining is a real but narrow, expensive defense.} At a
well-chosen budget it measurably hardens the detector against \S\ref{sec:adversarial}'s specific
attack -- median evasion effort rises from needing a third of the evasion range to needing nearly
all of it. But every budget that helps costs 8-10 points of clean balanced accuracy, driven by
recall, meaning a hardened deployment would silently miss more real, unperturbed ransomware in
exchange for resisting an adversary sophisticated enough to know the top-5 features and their
physical bounds. And the mechanism behind the gain -- learning that near-bound feature values are
themselves suspicious -- is exactly the kind of narrow, augmentation-shaped pattern that a
differently-shaped attack (partial perturbation of a \emph{different} feature subset, or of the
newly-important \texttt{directory\_breadth}/\texttt{entropy\_delta}/\texttt{key\_generation\_events})
was never tested against and has no particular reason to survive. Whether this trade-off is worth
taking is a deployment decision this project can inform but not make: it depends on whether an
operator weighs a sophisticated feature-space adversary more heavily than routine, unperturbed
ransomware, which this framework's own honeypot corpus (DEV-27) was built to represent as the
common case. The more durable fix implied by both \S\ref{sec:adversarial} and this section is
architectural, not just training a wider draw -- ensemble diversity over feature subsets, or a
loss that penalises reliance on any single small feature group, neither of which this session's
scope covers.

\subsection{Exact Minimum Adversarial Perturbation}
\label{sec:m7-17}

\S\ref{sec:adversarial}'s adaptive-evasion search finds the minimum perturbation fraction that
flips each row by a 101-point grid plus bisection to $10^{-4}$ -- an approximation that can miss a
narrow flip region between grid points. \textbf{Why the approximation error matters}: the 10th
percentile is the number an examiner remembers as "the fraction of ransomware trivially evadable,"
and \S\ref{sec:m7-8}'s retraining conclusions rest on comparing adaptive-evasion medians across
budgets -- if the approximation is loose, both could be wrong in either direction.

\textbf{Method.} $DM_{CSl}$'s decision is nearest-profile membership on the soft-vote average of
\emph{four} heterogeneous models (\texttt{random\_forest}, \texttt{decision\_tree},
\texttt{logistic\_regression}, \texttt{k\_nearest\_neighbours}), not a random-forest-only
majority vote. Three admit an exact treatment along the same ray \S\ref{sec:adversarial} already
searches: each tree's predicted class is piecewise-constant in the perturbation fraction $t$, with
breakpoints exactly where a moving feature crosses a split threshold on the sample's current
decision path (found by walking the tree and re-deriving the path after every crossing); the
logistic model's decision boundary is affine in $t$, solved in closed form. The fourth,
\texttt{k\_nearest\_neighbours}, would require enumerating pairwise crossings between the query's
distance curve and all $\sim$1467 training points -- $O(n_{\text{train}}^2)$ per sample -- judged
disproportionate to this session's time budget and not attempted. The exact
\texttt{random\_forest}/\texttt{decision\_tree}/\texttt{logistic\_regression} breakpoints are
merged with a dense grid (finer than the original 101 points) to bound any residual
\texttt{k\_nearest\_neighbours}-only crossing, then bisected to $10^{-6}$ -- by construction never
looser than the original search.

\textbf{Updated percentiles: the approximation error was small, and \S\ref{sec:adversarial}'s
conclusions hold.} Exact median \textbf{0.306} vs.\ the original search's \textbf{0.321}
(-1.5 points); p10/p90 move by under 0.2 points. 65 of 353 rows (18.4\%) are proven-optimal exact
results; the original search was $\geq$10 percentage points loose for only 4 of 353 rows (1.1\%),
all \texttt{k\_nearest\_neighbours}-driven. None of the 28 rows that never flip under the original
search actually flip under exact search -- that floor is confirmed, not a coarse-grid artifact.
Figure~\ref{fig:m7-17-histogram} overlays both distributions; they are visually almost identical,
consistent with the small measured shift.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figs/fig_m7_17_exact_vs_binary_search_histogram.png}
\caption{Minimum perturbation to evade, original detector: the original 101-point-grid search
vs.\ the exact/near-exact method. The distributions are close, matching the 1.5-point median
shift.}
\label{fig:m7-17-histogram}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Implications for \S\ref{sec:m7-8}: the 25\%/50\%-budget findings are unchanged; the
100\%-budget "never flips" claim needed correcting, not overturning.} Re-measuring \S\ref{sec:m7-8}'s
three hardened models with the exact method: the 25\%-budget backfire and 50\%-budget
bound-memorisation medians match the original search to under 0.01 percentage points. The
100\%-budget model's reported median of \textbf{1.000} ("never flips") is technically wrong but
practically right: exact search finds 177 of 353 rows (50.1\%) \emph{do} flip -- an 80.95-point
median shift, exceeding this session's 5-point retraining-revisit threshold. Verified directly
against the real ensemble (not the search machinery itself), 176 of those 177 revert to the
correct verdict one perturbation step later -- median dip width \textbf{0.38\%} of the evasion
range, versus \textbf{39.6\%} for the real flips the original (unhardened) model shows. The
100\%-budget model's decision surface is riddled with hundreds of one-sample-wide, unexploitable
adversarial windows near the augmented boundary, not a stable evasion region: no rational attacker
in \S\ref{sec:adversarial}'s threat model (tuning observable behaviour, not gradient access to
this exact fitted forest) could land inside a window roughly 1 part in 250 wide. This is direct,
quantitative evidence \emph{for} \S\ref{sec:m7-8}'s own "memorises the evasion boundary, not a
deeper representation" reading -- a genuinely smooth, generalising decision function does not have
that many razor-thin holes in it -- not evidence against it. No new retraining was run despite the
shift crossing the revisit threshold: the existing 100\%-budget model was not retrained or
altered, the shift is fully explained by measurement precision, and \S\ref{sec:m7-8}'s
\emph{practical} robustness conclusion is unchanged. Figure~\ref{fig:m7-8-pareto} is deliberately
not redrawn from the raw exact-median numbers alone -- plotting the 100\%-budget point at
"robustness 0.190" would be numerically true but would misrepresent the trade-off to a reader who
does not also see the stability breakdown above; a stability-adjusted robustness metric (minimum
\emph{stable} perturbation, under which the 100\%-budget point would sit far to the robust end,
since only 1 of its 178 real flips is stable) is a natural follow-up this session does not build.

\subsubsection{Neural vs.\ Tree-Based Detector: Architecture or Features?}
\label{sec:m7-10b}

\S\ref{sec:m7-8}'s memorisation failure mode (the 100\%-budget model learns ``values near the
evasion bound'' well enough that its combined-evasion curve \emph{rises} with perturbation) --
is that a property of tree ensembles' axis-aligned splits, or of the feature space and
augmentation strategy, independent of architecture? This section runs the identical protocol --
same top-5 features and evasion bounds, same training budgets, same downstream
detector/adversarial/retraining machinery -- against a small MLP (\texttt{detection.mlp\_model},
DEV-36: two hidden layers of 32/16 units, \texttt{adam}, L2 regularisation and early stopping)
plugged into the same \emph{NProf}/\emph{AProf} profiles as a single-model ensemble. Feature
importance is compared by permutation (an MLP has no Gini-style importance), so \S\ref{sec:m7-8}'s
Gini-based top-5 sums are not directly comparable even for the same RF model, recomputed here
like-for-like.

\textbf{The headline result: the MLP shows \S\ref{sec:m7-8}'s memorisation failure mode earlier
and more completely than RF, not less.} Every one of its three hardened combined-degradation
curves \emph{rises} monotonically with perturbation -- 25\%: 0.828$\to$0.877; 50\%:
0.810$\to$0.897; 100\%: 0.770$\to$0.896 -- the same signature \S\ref{sec:m7-8} found only at RF's
100\% budget, here at every budget tested. Its median adaptive-evasion perturbation jumps to
1.0000 at 25\% and stays there (Table~\ref{tab:m7-10b-adaptive}), and critically \textbf{it never
reproduces RF's 25\%-budget backfire} (RF's median evasion effort \emph{drops} to 0.2499 before
recovering; the MLP's never dips). A higher-capacity, continuously-weighted model does not avoid
the augmentation-boundary-memorisation failure mode -- it engages that shortcut faster and more
uniformly than an ensemble of axis-aligned trees does.

\textbf{Feature-importance shift, on different schedules.} By permutation importance
(Figure~\ref{fig:m7-10b-importance}), the MLP's top-5 reliance falls monotonically and sharply
with budget -- 0.0971 $\to$ 0.0328 $\to$ 0.0251 $\to$ 0.0219, a 77\% reduction end to end. RF's own
permutation-importance is \emph{non}-monotonic: 0.1170 $\to$ 0.1535 (a rise at 25\%) $\to$ 0.0818
$\to$ 0.0177 -- consistent with, and helping explain, RF's 25\%-budget backfire: by this measure
the narrowly-hardened RF becomes \emph{more} reliant on the attacked features at exactly the
budget where it is also most fragile. Neither model solves robustness by discarding the top-5
entirely, matching \S\ref{sec:m7-8}'s ``partial, not total'' finding and extending it to the MLP.

\textbf{Conclusion: the fragility is in the features and the augmentation strategy, not the tree
structure.} A tree-ensemble-specific artefact would predict a distributed neural representation
responding differently to the same augmentation; it does the opposite, adopting the rising-curve
signature at every budget including the one where RF instead backfires by a different mechanism.
Both failure modes point to the same root cause: augmenting positives toward a bounded region of a
fixed, low-dimensional feature space gives any sufficiently flexible model a shortcut easier to
learn than the underlying behavioural signal. The MLP's one genuine architectural advantage is
orthogonal to hardening (a smoother decision boundary, already harder to evade before any
retraining); the fix this section implies is the same one \S\ref{sec:m7-8} already named --
diversifying what the augmentation teaches -- not a change of model family.

\subsection{Real-Data Transfer: ClaMP, EMBER, and MalbehavD-V1}
\label{sec:m7-7}

\textbf{Why real-data evaluation matters.} M4b's 0.8422 balanced accuracy
(\S\ref{sec:honeypot-eval}) is the detection ensemble scored on \texttt{honeypot.collector}'s own
synthetic corpus -- the same session designed the generator and fit the classifier against it, so
that number measures the generator-detector \emph{pair}, not whether the detector would recognise
anything the generator did not anticipate. This section asks the question synthetic-only
evaluation cannot answer: does the detector transfer to real behavioural features it has never
seen, from programs it was not designed around? The detector used below is the identical fitted
ensemble -- same seed, same committed corpus, never retrained.

\textbf{Dataset.} The session brief named EMBER, CIC-MalMem-2022/CICMalDroid-2020 and
MalwareBazaar, in that order. EMBER's actual feature dataset is a $\sim$2.4GB archive; this
sandbox's measured throughput on a small test fetch ($\sim$40.6KB/s) projects a $\sim$17-hour
download, infeasible in one session. The CIC datasets are gated behind a research-access request
form, not a scriptable download. Used instead: \textbf{ClaMP} (Classification of Malware with PE
headers, \texttt{github.com/urwithajit9/ClaMP}) -- 5210 real Windows PE files' static
header-derived structural features, 2722 malicious and 2488 benign, openly published for exactly
this kind of ML research use. Fetched once and committed at \texttt{data/external/clamp/} (1.28MB,
sha256 recorded in its README) for full reproducibility, and used for \textbf{evaluation only}: it
never fits or tunes anything in \texttt{honeypot/} or \texttt{detection/}.

\textbf{The mapping table is itself the first finding.} $FT_{RW}$ (\S\ref{sec:honeypot}) is a
\emph{dynamic behavioural} schema -- what a monitor observes a running program \emph{do}. ClaMP is
\emph{static} -- header fields read off a file that is never executed. Table~\ref{tab:m77-mapping}
shows what that mismatch produces: only the entropy group (3 of 22 features) has any real
correspondence at all, because entropy is one of the few observables measurable both statically (a
file's own byte distribution) and dynamically (the byte distribution of what a process writes).
The other six groups -- filesystem, crypto API, process, network, persistence, kill-chain -- have
\textbf{no} analogue in a dataset that never ran the sample, and are marked missing, handled
exactly as \texttt{honeypot.features.build()} already handles an unobserved sensor: value $0.0$,
\texttt{missing\_mask} bit set.

\textbf{Limitations.} The mapping is necessarily imperfect: static header fields are the closest
real analogue available for entropy, and no real analogue exists at all for six of seven feature
groups, so the effective comparison the detector faces is far narrower than the 22-dimensional
space it was fit on. ClaMP's own labelling process (VirusTotal-report-based, per its own
documentation) may differ in criteria and era from the ransomware-specific threat model
$FT_{RW}$'s kill-chain groups were designed around -- ClaMP's malicious class is general malware,
not specifically ransomware. The transfer gap measured here is therefore a statement about how far
our generator's approximation reaches when most of its feature space cannot be exercised at all,
not a general claim about honeypot-based ransomware detection.

\textbf{A richer static dataset: EMBER.} EMBER2018 (\texttt{github.com/elastic/ember}) exposes far

and read/write/execute flags, and a full per-DLL import table -- and \texttt{honeypot.
ember\_mapping} (DEV-35) grounds 6 of 22 $FT_{RW}$ features against it (entropy 3/3, crypto\_api
2/3, filesystem 1/5), double ClaMP's 3/22, verified line-by-line against the \texttt{elastic/ember}
extractor source rather than assumed. The dataset (200,000 fully-labelled real PE files,
sha256-verified) was scored against the identical fitted ensemble, evaluation-only, same posture
as ClaMP.

\subsection{Multi-Family Ransomware Detection}
\label{sec:m7-16}

\S VII evaluates BitcoinHeist as a binary ransomware/benign problem. Its \texttt{label} column
already carries \emph{which} of 28 named families an address belongs to -- information the binary
evaluation (\S\ref{sec:5a} onward) never uses. Family identification matters for Algorithm 4:
mitigation dispatch is currently label-blind, but different ransomware families imply different
decryption tools and kill-chain speeds, so a detector that can name the family (not just flag
``ransomware'') is a precondition for differentiated response. This subsection tests whether the
same 8 address-level graph features that support the binary boundary also support 28+1 separate
ones -- natural class balance (the 90/10 resample has no 29-class analogue), rare families (fewer
than 10 samples, 11 of 28) merged into \texttt{other\_ransomware}, grouped stratified holdout,
full 2{,}916{,}697 rows, all four Table~II algorithms (logistic regression as one-vs-rest, per the
brief).

\textbf{The class imbalance is itself a finding.} \texttt{white}=2{,}875{,}284 (98.58\%), the
largest family \texttt{paduaCryptoWall}=12{,}390, three families with exactly one address each. 19
classes survive the merge; no family with $\geq$10 samples is folded in.

\textbf{Measured: macro F1 0.058--0.199 -- weak, real signal, not strong.} Weighted F1
(0.978--0.985) and top-3 accuracy (0.993--0.998) are both dominated by \texttt{white}'s 98.58\%
share and say little on their own; macro F1 is the metric that answers the question, and decision
tree is the best of the four (0.1985), narrowly over random forest (0.1978). Logistic regression's
one-vs-rest wrapper is worst by a wide margin (0.0584) and reproduces binary LR's exact
natural-rate failure -- it predicts nothing for every one-vs-rest sub-problem, the same collapse
\S\ref{sec:5b} already measured for the binary task.

\section{Scalability Analysis}
\label{sec:scalability}

Two questions the paper's own \S VII benchmarks timing but never asks: how much on-chain storage
does this design consume at realistic scale, and does its reported throughput actually reflect a
system that scales.

\subsection{Storage Cost (GAP-2)}
\label{sec:storage}

Section VII benchmarks consensus timing but never asks how much storage its own design consumes,
or at what scale that stops being feasible. We compute this from values already measured
elsewhere in this project -- the declared 4096B transaction payload (DEV-15), the measured 215B
framing and 161B hybrid-encryption overhead per chunk (DEV-24), and the measured 1.14$\times$
full-block overhead factor (header, Merkle root and canonical encoding, amortised over a 100-tx
block, \S\ref{sec:5c}) -- rather than from new estimates; full arithmetic in
\texttt{docs/STORAGE\_ANALYSIS.md}.

At hospital scale, the paper's own on-chain design fills 1~TiB per replica in about six months
under daily backups; at network scale, in under three weeks. Backup frequency is the larger lever
of the two the paper leaves open: at the medium scenario, daily backups cost $\approx$7$\times$
weekly and $\approx$30$\times$ monthly in chain growth for identical clinical content, a bigger
effect than DEV-15's payload-size sweep found from a 16$\times$ range of payload sizes alone.

Against this, an on-chain-hash / off-chain-data design -- store only a content digest and a
storage pointer on-chain, keep the encrypted payload in external storage -- preserves the same
tamper-evidence the paper's Merkle-root-and-signature chain already provides (recomputing and
comparing the digest catches off-chain tampering exactly as the paper's own immutability argument
requires), at $\approx$504~bytes per backup regardless of payload size, against
$\approx$1.14~MiB for a 1~MiB backup under the paper's design -- a $\approx$2{,}371$\times$
reduction that widens further as backups get larger, since the hash-only cost is flat and the
paper's is not. What it does not preserve is on-chain \emph{availability}: lose the off-chain
store and the digest alone cannot reconstruct the backup, where the paper's design always can.
We state this trade-off rather than resolving it, per this project's rule against silently
revising the paper's design.

Compute is not the bottleneck this analysis surfaces: the large-network scenario's daily block
count, at \S\ref{sec:5c}'s measured marginal per-block cost, comes to $\approx$4{,}045
CPU-seconds/day ($\approx$4.7\% of a day) on the same 8~GB dev laptop -- comfortably inside its
measured throughput ceiling, though nothing here measures a production cluster's network or disk
behaviour, only this one machine's compute.

\subsection{Throughput Does Not Scale With Chain Size (DEV-08)}
\label{sec:dev08}

\S\ref{sec:5c}'s measurement is the scalability-relevant fact directly: per-block marginal cost is
flat across cases 1 through 3 on both chains once cluster setup is excluded from the timed span
(Table~\ref{tab:marginal}), and derived TPS is correspondingly flat rather than rising. A design
whose own published throughput curve is the amortisation of a fixed setup cost over a growing
block count, not a property of the consensus mechanism, does not get faster as the deployment
grows -- the six published TPS values equal $\text{total\_tx}/\text{total\_time}$ exactly to four
significant figures, and removing the fixed cost from the timed span removes the rise, exactly
what an amortisation account predicts and what \S\ref{sec:5c-raft}'s independently-measured Raft
comparison confirms is not an artifact of pBFT specifically either.

\section{Critique}
\label{sec:critique}

Five defects, ordered by severity rather than by the order we found them. They are the ones
that rise to a methodological or architectural problem; a complete record of every smaller
departure from the paper's published design, each with its own rationale, is maintained
separately and is not reproduced here.

\subsection{FLAW-2 -- framework/evaluation disconnect}
\label{sec:flaw2}

Algorithm 2 collects data from a ransomware honeypot and Algorithm 3 trains a detector on the
resulting signatures and behavioural features -- properties of an executing, or emulated,
program. Section VII then evaluates entirely on BitcoinHeist: address-level graph features
(length, weight, count, looped, neighbours, income) of a Bitcoin address that has already
received a ransom payment. These are different problems. Detecting a running ransomware program
from its behaviour and forensically labelling a wallet address after the fact share no features,
no data-generating process, and arguably not even the same notion of a positive instance -- one
is prospective and behavioural, the other retrospective and financial. Nothing in \S IV-C or
\S VII acknowledges the substitution.

We built both pipelines rather than choosing one. The BitcoinHeist pipeline
(\S\ref{sec:5a}--\S\ref{sec:5b}) reproduces the paper's actual published evaluation. The
honeypot pipeline (\S\ref{sec:honeypot}, \S\ref{sec:honeypot-eval}) runs Algorithm 2's honeypot
output through real Phase-2 consensus onto $BC_{SigRW}$, decrypts it back out, and trains
Algorithm 3's four models on it -- the sequence the paper's own Fig.~3 draws. It produces a
real, bounded result (0.8422 balanced accuracy, \S\ref{sec:honeypot-eval}). The paper's
framework half, run end to end as specified, was never demonstrated by its authors; only the
mismatched evaluation half was. FLAW-2 is therefore sharper than ``the paper evaluates on the
wrong data'': the paper's own framework, when actually built and run, produces a defensible
result that the paper itself never reports, choosing an unrelated dataset instead.

\subsection{FLAW-4 -- evaluation methodology}

Section VII resamples BitcoinHeist to 90\% ransomware before evaluating, without stating that the
resample is bounded by the scarce class: only 41{,}413 ransomware rows exist in the cited
2{,}916{,}697-row dataset, so a 90\%-ransomware sample can hold at most
$41{,}413/0.9=46{,}014$ rows -- 1.6\% of the dataset described two paragraphs earlier. The
published 98.98\% accuracy is a number from a $\sim$46K-row experiment, reported with nothing to
mark that it is not the 2.9M-row one. Compounding this, the resulting class balance makes
accuracy nearly free: a constant-positive classifier on this split scores 90.0\%
(\S\ref{sec:5a}).

\S\ref{sec:5a} and \S\ref{sec:m76} measure the consequence directly: reproducing the paper's own
protocol exactly falls 4.56 accuracy points short of published, and an eight-hypothesis ablation
across two sessions -- the two most plausible leakage sources first, then splitting criterion,
resample strategy, single-split variance, feature engineering, and a programmatically-stacked
worst case -- narrows that bound to 3.58 and then 1.51 points without closing it at any tested
cell. We report the residual as unexplained, with a measured and considerably tightened bound,
rather than attribute it to a cause we found no evidence for.

\subsection{FLAW-5 -- the \S V-3 security claim}
\label{sec:flaw5}

Section V-3 argues that BSFR-SH resists 51\% attacks because it replaces proof-of-work with pBFT.
The argument borrows PoW's 51\% threshold and applies it to a consensus mechanism with a
different one: pBFT is safe only while fewer than $n/3$ of replicas are Byzantine
($n\geq3f+1$). Moving from PoW to pBFT does not raise the fraction of the network an adversary
must control -- it \emph{lowers} it, from just over one half to one third. At the paper's own
deployment of four miner nodes, $f=1$: two colluding replicas (50\% of the cluster -- above the
$f=1$ bound the cluster actually tolerates, and below the 51\% figure the paper cites as the
threshold being defended against) can make two honest replicas commit different blocks at the
same height. We demonstrate this directly rather than argue it: a test constructs exactly this
scenario against our own pBFT implementation and asserts the fork occurs, at exactly the bound
$f$ predicts and no tighter.

The fair counter-argument, which \S V-3 could have made and does not, is that pBFT's threshold
counts identities, not hash power, and in a permissioned deployment identities cannot be minted
by an outside adversary -- a property we test separately, showing a swarm of unauthorised
identities cannot form a certificate regardless of size. The defensible version of \S V-3's claim
is therefore \emph{operational} -- ``an attacker must compromise two of our four named cloud
servers'' -- not consensus-theoretic, and it gets \emph{weaker}, not stronger, in relative terms
as a reason to prefer pBFT over PoW at a fixed and small node count.

\subsection{DEV-26 -- preprocessing deletes the positive class}
\label{sec:flaw-preprocess}

Algorithm 2 line 3 pre-processes honeypot data and, in its entirety, ``removes the
abnormalities.'' The literal reading is statistical outlier removal. On a corpus whose purpose is
teaching a detector what ransomware looks like, that reading is self-defeating: ransomware
behaviour is the minority class by construction -- it \emph{is} the abnormality -- so an
outlier-removal step deletes exactly the samples the detector exists to learn from. Applied
naively, Algorithm 2's own pre-processing step would hand Algorithm 3 a clean-looking dataset
with the positive class silently gone, and \S VII would then report a plausible-looking accuracy
computed on data that never saw an attack. We interpret ``abnormalities'' as sensor defects
rather than unusual behaviour (\S\ref{sec:preprocessing}) and pin the alternative reading's
failure mode with a direct test asserting that cleaning never removes the positive class.

\subsection{DEV-08 -- TPS is Amortisation, not Throughput}

Section VII-D presents TPS as a measured throughput property that improves as the chain grows
across the paper's three cases. It is not: every published TPS value equals
$\text{total\_tx}/\text{total\_time}$ exactly, and \S\ref{sec:dev08} measures the direct
consequence -- once cluster setup is excluded from the timed span, marginal per-block cost and
derived TPS are both flat, exactly what "the published rise is amortisation" predicts and what
our own implementation lets us test directly rather than infer.

\section{What the Paper Gets Right}

Two of the paper's choices are execution defects, not premise defects, and the distinction
matters for how it should be read. The two-chain architecture -- one chain for the ground truth a
detector trains on, a separate chain for the backups a system restores from, never sharing node
sets or state -- genuinely does separate detection integrity from recovery integrity: an
adversary who compromises the mechanism protecting one does not thereby compromise the other. And
the central idea that blockchain immutability can protect a detector's training data from
tampering, in a domain where an attacker has every incentive to poison exactly that data if they
can reach it, is sound, and is not the load-bearing idea of any of the four papers Table~II
compares against. The paper's execution of that idea -- its evaluation methodology, its security
argument for choosing pBFT, its preprocessing specification, and the connection between what it
builds and what it measures -- has the five gaps above. Both are true, and a critique that lists
only the five would misrepresent the paper's contribution as smaller than it is.

\section{Conclusion}

We built a complete, from-scratch implementation of BSFR-SH's five algorithms, both of its
independent private blockchains under real pBFT consensus, and both a faithful reproduction of
the paper's published evaluation and the honeypot feature pipeline its own Phase 2/3 requires but
never defines. Reproducing the paper's own methodology exactly, its headline detection result
does not reproduce: our best model reaches 0.9442/0.9697 against a published 0.9898/0.990, and an
eight-hypothesis ablation across two follow-up sessions narrows, without closing, a gap that
tightens from 4.56 to 3.58 to 1.51 accuracy points. We find five further, independent defects --
a disconnect between what the framework is designed to detect and what it is evaluated on, a
degenerate and undisclosed evaluation split, a security argument that inverts pBFT's actual
threshold relative to proof-of-work, a preprocessing specification that, read literally, deletes
the class it exists to detect, and a reported throughput curve that is arithmetic amortisation
rather than a measured property. Against this, the paper's central architectural idea -- two
chains, separately protecting a detector's ground truth and a system's recoverability, from an
adversary with every incentive to attack both -- is sound, and our own honeypot pipeline shows the
framework can produce a real, bounded detection result (0.8422 balanced accuracy) once its
undefined layer is actually built.

Fifteen extensions, built once that working system existed, tested the architectural idea itself
rather than the paper's published numbers, across three analyses. \textbf{Security}
(\S\ref{sec:security}): the four-tier threat model \S V never states; formal (Scyther)
verification of our own session protocol (Tier 1); a pBFT-vs-Raft comparison finding a single
Byzantine leader corrupts Raft's chain undetectably, which is the reason pBFT, not Raft, is the
correct choice here -- a reason the paper never states (Tier 2); external anchoring against a
simulated public chain, answering \S VIII's own stated future work (Tier 2--3); and a real,
measured honeypot data-poisoning attack together with three independent defenses built and
evaluated against it, closing the loop this project's own threat model opened. The statistical
defense's finding is a genuine research contribution, not a critique: it catches the poisoning
strategy predicted least catchable (an engineered class-boundary midpoint that, in a balanced
corpus, coincides with the population mean) and misses the one predicted most catchable (label
flipping, invisible to a feature-only detector by construction), at a false-positive cost measured
rather than assumed. The protocol-level commit-then-reveal defense (\S\ref{sec:m7-14}), built
specifically to close that missed case, behaves exactly as designed -- yet measurably does not
degrade anchor-point injection's damage on our corpus, because the corpus's stationary,
low-dimensional profiles let a historical-only centroid estimate converge almost as fast as a
full-information one; a correctly-built defense whose real-world reach turned out narrower than
its own motivating argument, measured rather than assumed either way. A third, federated defense
(\S\ref{sec:m7-15}) exploits the paper's own replicated cloud-server architecture instead of
inspecting or restricting data, costing nothing beyond what that architecture already provides --
architecturally the cleanest of the three, but on our corpus only narrowly and inconsistently
useful: it correctly identifies a poisoner in every tested cell, but the disagreement it flags is
the \emph{correct} model, not the wrong one, for the one strategy the other two defenses also
struggled with. \textbf{Detection}
(\S\ref{sec:detection-analysis}): the honeypot pipeline's own
end-to-end result; adversarial feature-space evasion finding roughly a quarter of ransomware
samples trivially evadable, and a hardening defense that works only at a real, non-trivial
accuracy cost and only within a narrow training-budget window, confirmed non-tree-specific by an
independent neural-network replication; and transfer evaluation against three real malware
datasets (ClaMP, EMBER, and the dynamic MalbehavD-V1) finding that even a dataset grounding 12/22
features across 5/7 groups -- including the process and network groups no static dataset can touch
-- still fails to transfer, a scale-calibration mismatch between the generator's assumed-unbounded
observation window and a real bounded sandbox trace, not an absence of real dynamic signal.
\textbf{Scalability} (\S\ref{sec:scalability}): on-chain storage growth from clinical to
network scale, and confirmation that the paper's own reported throughput curve does not scale
the way it is presented as scaling. One extension remains unpursued: an asynchronous pBFT variant
with modelled network latency beyond the fixed delays \S IV-C already sweeps.

Taken together, the three findings point in the same direction rather than opposite ones. A paper
whose evaluation methodology cannot be checked because its protocol is underspecified, whose one
checkable headline number does not reproduce even under the most literal reading of its own
stated split, and whose central architectural idea -- once actually built, attacked, and
defended -- turns out to have real, measured gaps its own security argument has no vocabulary to
name, is a paper whose claims should not be relied on as published, at any of the three levels
this report checked them. That is a narrower conclusion than ``the framework does not work'': our
own honeypot pipeline shows a version of Algorithm 3, trained on data Algorithm 2 actually
specifies producing, detecting ransomware at a real, bounded, non-degenerate rate, and our own
statistical defense against the poisoning attack this project also found shows that at least one
of the architecture's real gaps is partially solvable, measured rather than merely named -- while
the protocol-level defense built for the case the statistical one misses shows that "correctly
built" and "measurably protective" are not the same claim, and a project honest with itself
reports both. The architecture is worth building on. The published numbers, as published, and the
informal security argument, as argued, are not evidence that it works or defends as well as
claimed.
